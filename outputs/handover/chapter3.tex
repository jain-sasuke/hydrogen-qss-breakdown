\chapter{Two Clocks}
\label{ch:theory}

Chapter~\ref{ch:atoms} established what can happen to a single hydrogen atom in
a plasma and how fast each process runs. This chapter asks the next question: if
we know what happens to one atom, how do we follow $10^{12}$ or more of them per
cubic centimetre, distributed over 43 energy levels, and what makes the answer
hard?

The answer to the second half is that the system has two clocks running at
once, separated by four orders of magnitude. Everything in this thesis follows
from that separation --- including, as Chapter~\ref{ch:results} shows, a result
that the separation itself does not predict.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{What this chapter takes as given.} Three things are established
elsewhere and used here without re-derivation. From Chapter~\ref{ch:intro}: that
divertor conditions are inferred spectroscopically, that the Balmer
$H_\alpha/H_\beta$ ratio is the working diagnostic, and the operating range
$\Te = 1$--\SI{10}{\electronvolt}, $\nel = 10^{12}$--\SI{e15}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}
over which everything is computed. From Chapter~\ref{ch:atoms}: the 43-level
state space and the reason $\ell$ is resolved for $n \le 8$ and bundled above it;
the origin and accuracy of every rate coefficient; and the Maxwellian averaging
that turns a cross section into a rate. This chapter assumes those rates exist
and are correct, and asks only what the resulting equations do.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{The rate equation}
\label{sec:rate_equation}

\subsection{A balance on every level}

Consider one energy level of the hydrogen atom --- say $2p$ --- and count atoms.
Atoms arrive in $2p$ when an electron collision excites a ground-state atom into
it, when an atom in a higher level decays into it radiatively, when a collision
knocks an atom down from above, and when a proton captures an electron directly
into it. Atoms leave $2p$ when they are excited upward, knocked downward,
ionised, or radiate to $1s$.

The rate of change of the $2p$ population is the sum of everything arriving
minus the sum of everything leaving. That is all a rate equation is.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{fig3_4_partition.pdf}
  \caption[The collisional--radiative network and its slow/fast partition]{%
  The collisional--radiative network of atomic hydrogen and the partition used
  in Section~\ref{sec:qss}. Levels with $n \leq 8$ are resolved in orbital
  angular momentum; $n = 9$--$15$ are bundled with a statistical
  $\ell$-distribution. Proton-impact $\ell$-mixing (orange) couples sublevels
  within a shell and is the dominant loss channel for the metastable $2s$
  level, which has no electric-dipole route to the ground state. The broad
  arrows are not elementary transitions: $\mathbf{a} =
  -\Lmat_{FF}^{-1}\Lmat_{FS}$ and $\mathbf{c} = -\Lmat_{FF}^{-1}\Svec_F$ are
  \emph{network responses}, each summing every cascade route from its
  reservoir into a given level (Section~\ref{sec:R_depends_on_b}). Vertical
  spacing is schematic and not drawn to energy scale.}
  \label{fig:network}
\end{figure}

A chemical engineer has written this equation many times. It is a species
balance on a well-mixed vessel, with one species per energy level and one
reaction per atomic process. Figure~\ref{fig:network} shows the network and the
partition that Section~\ref{sec:qss} will exploit. The only unusual feature is
the number of species --- 43 --- and the fact that some of the ``reactions'' are radiative and
therefore proceed without a collision partner.

Write it out for a general level $p$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathrm{d}n_p}{\mathrm{d}t}
  = \underbrace{\sum_{q \neq p} C_{qp}\,\nel\,n_q}_{\text{collisions in}}
  - \underbrace{\sum_{q \neq p} C_{pq}\,\nel\,n_p}_{\text{collisions out}}
  + \underbrace{\sum_{q > p} A_{qp}\,n_q}_{\text{radiation in}}
  - \underbrace{\gamma_p\,n_p}_{\text{radiation out}}
  - \underbrace{Q_p\,\nel\,n_p}_{\text{ionisation}}
  + \underbrace{S_p\,\nion}_{\text{recombination}}
  \label{eq:level_balance}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $n_p$ is the population of level $p$ in \si{\per\cubic\centi\metre};
$C_{qp}$ is the rate coefficient for the collisional transition $q \to p$ in
\si{\cubic\centi\metre\per\second}; $A_{qp}$ is the Einstein coefficient for
spontaneous emission $q \to p$ in \si{\per\second}; $\gamma_p = \sum_{q<p}A_{pq}$
is the total radiative loss rate from $p$; $Q_p$ is the ionisation rate
coefficient out of $p$ in \si{\cubic\centi\metre\per\second}; and $S_p$ is the
recombination source per unit ion density in \si{\per\second}, given in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:source} below.

Collisional terms carry a density factor because a collision requires a
partner; radiative terms do not, because an excited atom decays whether or not
anything else is nearby. Which partner matters: electron-impact terms scale with
$\nel$, proton-impact terms with $\nion$. For the pure hydrogen plasma
considered here quasi-neutrality gives $\nion = \nel$, so both scale together and
the distinction is one of physics rather than of parametric dependence. This is the first hint of the
two clocks: radiative rates are fixed by atomic structure, collisional rates
scale with density, and the competition between them is what makes the answer
depend on where in the plasma you look.

\subsection{Matrix form}

Equation~\eqref{eq:level_balance} holds for each of the 43 levels. Stacking
them and collecting the terms that are linear in the tracked populations gives
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\ \frac{\mathrm{d}\nvec}{\mathrm{d}t} = \Lmat\,\nvec + \Svec\,\nion\ }
  \label{eq:master}
\end{equation}

\noindent with $\nvec$ the 43-component population vector
(\si{\per\cubic\centi\metre}), $\Lmat$ the collisional--radiative rate matrix
(\si{\per\second}), and $\Svec\nion$ the recombination feed
(\si{\per\cubic\centi\metre\per\second}). The matrix elements are
\begin{align}
  L_{pq} &= C^{(e)}_{qp}\,\nel + C^{(i)}_{qp}\,\nion + A_{qp}
            \qquad (p \neq q),
  \label{eq:L_offdiag}\\
  L_{pp} &= -\left(\sum_{q\neq p}
            \left[C^{(e)}_{pq}\,\nel + C^{(i)}_{pq}\,\nion\right]
            + \gamma_p + Q_p\,\nel\right).
  \label{eq:L_diag}
\end{align}

The superscripts distinguish the two collision partners. $C^{(e)}$ denotes
electron-impact transitions and is the dominant term for changes of principal
quantum number. $C^{(i)}$ denotes proton-impact transitions, which in this model
are non-zero only for $\ell$-changing collisions within a shell
($\Delta n = 0$, $\Delta\ell = \pm1$, $n \le 8$). That term is easy to overlook
and it is not optional. Chapter~\ref{ch:atoms} shows it carries
\SI{99.92}{\percent} of the total loss rate from the metastable $2s$ level.
The reason is that $2s$ has no other fast exit: the transition $2s \to 1s$ is
forbidden to electric-dipole radiation, and no two-photon rate is included in
the present radiative dataset, so the only route out other than proton
$\ell$-mixing is electron-impact de-excitation, some three orders of magnitude
slower. Removing proton $\ell$-mixing therefore does not merely perturb the
$n=2$ manifold; it changes which process controls it. Quasi-neutrality gives
$\nion = \nel$ for the pure hydrogen plasma considered here, so both collisional
terms scale with the same density.

\noindent Note the index order: $L_{pq}$ with $p \neq q$ is the rate of the
transition $q \to p$, a \emph{gain} into $p$. Off-diagonal elements are
therefore non-negative and diagonal elements are negative. This convention is
the opposite of the one some texts use, and getting it backwards transposes the
physics; it is stated here once and used throughout.

\subsection{Why recombination is a feed stream, not a matrix element}

There is one term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:level_balance} that refuses to become a
matrix element, and understanding why is worth a paragraph.

$\Lmat$ multiplies $\nvec$. Every entry of $\Lmat\nvec$ must therefore be
proportional to the population of some \emph{tracked} level. Excitation,
de-excitation, radiative decay and ionisation all satisfy this: they take an
atom that is in the manifold and move it, or remove it. Recombination does
not. It is proportional to $\nel\nion$ --- the density of a species outside the
neutral manifold entirely --- and so it enters as a constant feed, independent of
$\nvec$.

The chemical-engineering analogy is exact and worth holding onto for the rest of
the thesis. $\Lmat$ is the reaction-rate matrix of a network of 43 species in a
well-mixed vessel. $\Svec\nion$ is the inlet stream. And the ion population is
the reservoir feeding that inlet --- a quantity this model does not evolve, which
becomes important in Section~\ref{sec:two_references} and again in
Chapter~\ref{ch:limits}.

\subsection{Units, and a source that is quadratic in density}

The source deserves care because its units are easy to state wrongly. Writing
it out,
\begin{equation}
  \Svec = \nel\left(\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{RR}}
        + \nel\,\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{3BR}}\right)
  \label{eq:source}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{RR}}$ is the radiative recombination
coefficient (\si{\cubic\centi\metre\per\second}) and
$\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{3BR}}$ the three-body coefficient
(\si{\centi\metre\tothe{6}\per\second}). Three-body recombination requires a
second electron to carry away the excess energy, so it carries an extra factor
of $\nel$; the source as a whole is therefore \emph{quadratic} in electron
density, and $\Svec$ has units of \si{\per\second}, not
\si{\cubic\centi\metre\per\second}.

This matters physically, not just dimensionally. At $\nel = \SI{e12}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$
the three-body channel supplies under 10\% of the feed into any level; at
$\nel = \SI{e15}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ it supplies more than 99\% into the
high-$n$ shells. The same equation therefore describes two physically different
regimes at the two ends of the density grid, a fact used in
Chapter~\ref{ch:validation} to test the two recombination datasets separately.

Dimensional check on Eq.~\eqref{eq:master}: $[\Lmat\nvec] =
\si{\per\second}\cdot\si{\per\cubic\centi\metre} =
\si{\per\cubic\centi\metre\per\second}$, and
$[\Svec\nion] = \si{\per\second}\cdot\si{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, matching
$[\mathrm{d}\nvec/\mathrm{d}t]$. Every term is a number of atoms per unit volume
per unit time, as a balance equation must be.

\section{Conservation, and how we know the matrix is right}
\label{sec:conservation}

A $43\times43$ matrix assembled from six separate atomic datasets offers a great
many opportunities to make a mistake that produces plausible numbers. Before
using $\Lmat$ for anything, we need a test that a wrong matrix would fail.

The test comes from counting atoms. Sum Eq.~\eqref{eq:master} over all levels
with the feed switched off. Every collisional and radiative transition moves an
atom \emph{within} the manifold: it is a loss from one level and a gain to
another, so the two contributions cancel in the sum. Only ionisation genuinely
removes an atom from the bound states. Therefore
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\ \sum_{p} L_{pq} = -Q_q\,\nel \qquad \text{for every column } q.\ }
  \label{eq:conservation}
\end{equation}

\noindent Each column of $\Lmat$ must sum to the ionisation leak from the level
that column represents --- no more, no less. An error in a single off-diagonal
element, a transposed index, or a missing back-reaction all break this
immediately.

\subsection{A worked example}

The rule is easiest to see in a two-level system. Take $1s$ and $2p$ only, at
$\Te = \SI{3}{\electronvolt}$ and $\nel = \SI{e14}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, with
representative coefficients $C_{12} = \SI{1e-11}{\cubic\centi\metre\per\second}$,
$C_{21} = \SI{1e-10}{\cubic\centi\metre\per\second}$,
$A_{21} = \SI{6.27e8}{\per\second}$,
$Q_1 = \SI{1e-13}{\cubic\centi\metre\per\second}$ and
$Q_2 = \SI{1e-10}{\cubic\centi\metre\per\second}$. Then
\begin{equation}
  \Lmat =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    -1010 & 6.2701\times10^{8}\\[2pt]
    1000  & -6.2702\times10^{8}
  \end{pmatrix}\si{\per\second},
  \qquad
  \sum_p L_{pq} = \begin{pmatrix}-10 \\ -10^{4}\end{pmatrix}\si{\per\second}
  \label{eq:toy_matrix}
\end{equation}

\noindent and $Q_1\nel = \SI{10}{\per\second}$, $Q_2\nel = \SI{e4}{\per\second}$.
The rule holds.

Two features of this small matrix survive into the full one and are worth
noticing now.

First, the second column is a difference of two numbers that agree to four
significant figures: $6.2701\times10^{8}$ against $6.2702\times10^{8}$, leaving
$10^{4}$. Almost all of the $2p$ loss rate is radiative decay to the ground
state, and the physically interesting part --- the ionisation leak --- is four
orders of magnitude smaller. Conservation is therefore always quoted as a
\emph{relative} residual; an absolute one would be meaningless.

Second, that same near-cancellation is the seed of the two clocks. The $2p$
level empties in nanoseconds by radiating, but the atom that arrives back in
$1s$ is still in the manifold. Only the small ionisation term actually removes
it. Section~\ref{sec:two_clocks} makes this quantitative.

\subsection{The measured residual}

For the full matrix, evaluated at all 400 grid points, the largest relative
column-sum residual is
\begin{equation}
  \max_{q,\,\Te,\,\nel}
  \left|\frac{\sum_p L_{pq} + Q_q\nel}{Q_q\nel}\right|
  = 3.61\times10^{-11},
  \label{eq:conservation_measured}
\end{equation}

\noindent which is round-off for a matrix whose entries span eight orders of
magnitude. The assembly is arithmetically sound.

It is worth being precise about what this does and does not establish. It
establishes that every transition entered as a loss also entered as a gain
somewhere. It says nothing whatever about whether the \emph{magnitude} of any
rate is correct: $\ell$-mixing, for instance, cancels exactly in the column sum
and so is invisible to this test. Checking magnitudes requires comparison
against independent sources, which is the subject of
Chapter~\ref{ch:validation}. A test that a wrong result would also have passed
is not a test.

\section{Two clocks}
\label{sec:two_clocks}

\subsection{Why the system has natural timescales at all}

Equation~\eqref{eq:master} is linear, so with $\Lmat$ and the feed held fixed its
solution is a steady state plus a sum of decaying exponentials:
\begin{equation}
  \nvec(t) = \nvec^{\mathrm{ss}}
           + \sum_{k} c_k\,\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_k t}\,\bm{v}_k,
  \qquad
  \Lmat\bm{v}_k = \lambda_k\bm{v}_k,
  \qquad
  \nvec^{\mathrm{ss}} = -\Lmat^{-1}\Svec\nion.
  \label{eq:eigen_solution}
\end{equation}

\noindent Each eigenvalue $\lambda_k$ has a negative real part --- the system is
stable, as a system of decaying populations must be --- and each contributes a
mode decaying with time constant $\tau_k = 1/|\lambda_k|$. The set of $\tau_k$
is the set of clocks the system runs on.

It is worth pausing on what Eq.~\eqref{eq:eigen_solution} has achieved, because
the whole chapter turns on it. In the populations $n_p$ the 43 balance equations
are hopelessly coupled: nothing happens to $3d$ without also happening to $2p$,
$4f$, and everything else. Diagonalising $\Lmat$ is a change of coordinates.
Instead of asking how many atoms are in each \emph{level}, ask how much of each
\emph{eigenvector} the state contains --- and in those coordinates the coupling
disappears entirely. The $k$th coordinate obeys $\dot{c}_k = \lambda_k c_k$,
which is the first-order decay $c_k(t) = c_k(0)\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_k}$ familiar
from single-step kinetics. Forty-three coupled equations become forty-three
independent ones, each with its own clock.

The eigenvectors are therefore not abstract: each is a \emph{pattern of
populations} that decays without changing shape, and any state of the system is
a superposition of those patterns fading at their own rates. Asking which
physical process a mode corresponds to is then a matter of reading which levels
carry its weight, which is what Section~\ref{sec:two_clocks} does next. One
caveat, developed in Section~\ref{sec:transient_growth}: this decomposition is
exact, but the patterns are not orthogonal, so a small state can be the sum of
two large and nearly cancelling ones.

For hydrogen at divertor conditions there are 43 of them. The striking fact,
and the one this thesis is built on, is that they are not spread out evenly.

next by a factor of ten thousand; after that the modes crowd together, each

structure --- it is one very slow mode sitting far above a dense quasi-continuum
of fast ones.

\subsection{What the two modes are}

The eigenvectors identify them.

The slow mode $\bm{v}_0$ is almost entirely ground state: its $1s$ component is
$1.0000$ and every excited component is below $10^{-3}$. Its participation ratio,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{PR}(\bm{v}) = \frac{\left(\sum_i |v_i|^2\right)^2}{\sum_i |v_i|^4},
  \label{eq:participation_ratio}
\end{equation}
which counts the effective number of states a mode involves --- unity for a mode
confined to a single level, $43$ for one spread evenly over all of them --- is
$1.00$. This mode is the relaxation of the
ground-state population against the prescribed ion reservoir. It is often
called the ionisation balance, and that is the right physical picture, but the
term should be used with care here: this model holds $\nion$ fixed
(Section~\ref{sec:rate_equation}), so what relaxes is $\ngs$ alone and not a
self-consistent neutral--ion partition. Chapter~\ref{ch:limits} assesses what
that costs.

Its eigenvalue is closely related to, but not equal to, the direct ionisation
rate of the ground state. Writing
\begin{equation}
  |\lambda_0| = \eta\,Q_{1s}\,\nel ,
  \label{eq:stepwise_enhancement}
\end{equation}

\noindent the factor $\eta$ ranges from $1.16$ to $29.5$ across the grid, and
equals $3.12$ at the benchmark point. It is nowhere below unity, and it is the
\emph{stepwise-ionisation enhancement}: ground-state atoms are not only ionised
directly but also excited into the manifold and ionised from there.
Equation~\eqref{eq:reduced} shows exactly this --- $\eta$ is the ratio of the
full Schur complement $\Omqss$ to its direct term $\Lmat_{SS}$. Consistently,
$\eta$ grows with density, where collisional climbing of the ladder is easier,
and falls with temperature, where direct ionisation is already fast: at
$\Te = \SI{10}{\electronvolt}$, $\nel = \SI{e12}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ it is
$1.16$, and at $\Te = \SI{1}{\electronvolt}$,
$\nel = \SI{e15}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ it is $29.5$ --- almost all ionisation
there proceeds stepwise. We write
\begin{equation}
  \tauqss \equiv \frac{1}{|\lambda_0|} = \SI{22.73}{\micro\second}.
\end{equation}

The next mode $\bm{v}_1$ has almost no ground-state weight and is distributed
across the excited manifold. It is the excited states rearranging among
themselves:
\begin{equation}
  \taurel \equiv \frac{1}{|\lambda_1|} = \SI{2.277}{\nano\second}.
\end{equation}

Their ratio is the \emph{separation}
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\ \Msep \equiv \frac{\tauqss}{\taurel} = 9982
  \quad\text{at the benchmark point.}\ }
  \label{eq:M_benchmark}
\end{equation}

The excited manifold settles ten thousand times faster than the ionisation
balance moves. Put in units a reader can hold: if the ionisation balance took a
year to equilibrate, the excited states would be done in under an hour.

\subsection{The separation across the operating range}

Table~\ref{tab:spectrum} is one grid point. Across all 400,
\begin{equation}
  \SI{0.87}{\nano\second} \le \taurel \le \SI{38.9}{\nano\second},
  \qquad
  \SI{1.18}{\micro\second} \le \tauqss \le \SI{67.2}{\second},
  \qquad
  86.8 \le \Msep \le 1.73\times10^{9}.
  \label{eq:M_range}
\end{equation}

\noindent $\taurel$ varies by a factor of 45; $\tauqss$ by seven orders of
magnitude. The separation is never small: even at its weakest --- the hottest,
densest corner of the grid --- the excited states are almost ninety times faster
than the ionisation balance.

$\taurel$ falls with density with a fitted logarithmic slope
$\mathrm{d}\ln\taurel/\mathrm{d}\ln\nel$ between $-0.46$ and $-0.53$ across the
temperature range, rather than the $-1$ that a purely collisional relaxation
would give. The difference is radiative decay,
which is density-independent and increasingly dominates the excited-state loss
budget as the density falls.

\subsection{Is the definition robust?}

Two definitions of $\taurel$ are available and it is worth checking they agree.
The first, used above, takes the second eigenvalue of the full $43\times43$
matrix. The second computes the slowest eigenvalue of the excited block
$\Lmat_{FF}$ alone --- that is, the relaxation of the excited manifold with the
ground state held fixed. These are different calculations and there is no
guarantee they coincide.

They agree to \SI{0.0098}{\percent} at the benchmark point and to better than
\SI{0.35}{\percent} everywhere on the grid. The excited manifold relaxes at
essentially the same rate whether or not the ground state is allowed to
participate --- which is a first, quantitative indication that the two subsystems
really are separable, and anticipates the formal argument of
Section~\ref{sec:qss}.

\subsection{What the separation does and does not license}

A separation of $10^{4}$ invites an obvious simplification: treat the excited
states as instantaneously equilibrated, and solve only for the slow subsystem.
That is the quasi-steady-state approximation, and Section~\ref{sec:qss} derives
it properly.

Two warnings, both of which matter later.

First, $\Msep$ compares two \emph{rates}. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} measures a
\emph{distance} --- how far a diagnostic reading sits from the truth. Knowing how
quickly a system recovers from a displacement tells you nothing about how far it
was displaced, and these are independent quantities.

Second, eigenvalues describe the long-time behaviour of each mode in isolation.
The matrix $\Lmat$ is strongly non-normal: its eigenvectors are far from
orthogonal, because radiative decay downward is some nine orders of magnitude
faster than collisional excitation upward. For such a matrix the modal
amplitudes can be large and opposing, and a perturbation can grow before it
decays even though every eigenvalue is negative. The spectrum bounds the
asymptotic decay; it does not bound the finite-time error.

Both warnings point the same way: the two clocks tell us the system \emph{can}
be simplified, and they say nothing about what the simplification costs. Working
that out is the business of the rest of this chapter.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{The quasi-steady-state approximation}
\label{sec:qss}

\subsection{The shortcut}

A separation of $10^{4}$ invites an obvious move. If the excited states settle
in nanoseconds while the ionisation balance takes microseconds, then on any
timescale relevant to the second, the first has already finished. So do not
solve for the excited states at all: assume they have reached whatever
configuration the current conditions demand, and solve only for the slow
subsystem.

This is the quasi-steady-state approximation, and every practical
collisional--radiative model uses some form of it. Without it, a plasma
simulation that resolves \si{\nano\second} timesteps in order to follow the
excited states cannot afford to run for the \si{\milli\second} an experiment
lasts.

The chemical-engineering version of this argument is the Bodenstein
steady-state approximation. For a reaction $A \to I \to P$ with a short-lived
intermediate, one sets $\mathrm{d}[I]/\mathrm{d}t = 0$ and solves algebraically
for $[I] = (\text{production})/(\text{loss})$. The intermediate stops being a
differential unknown and becomes slaved to whatever $[A]$ currently is. What
follows is the same move applied to 42 intermediates at once, coupled to each
other.

\subsection{Partition and elimination}

Split the 43 levels into a \emph{slow} subspace $\slow = \{1s\}$ and a
\emph{fast} subspace $\fast$ containing the 42 excited levels, and partition
Eq.~\eqref{eq:master} conformably:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}
  \begin{pmatrix}\nS\\[2pt]\nF\end{pmatrix}
  =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \Lmat_{SS} & \Lmat_{SF}\\[2pt]
    \Lmat_{FS} & \Lmat_{FF}
  \end{pmatrix}
  \begin{pmatrix}\nS\\[2pt]\nF\end{pmatrix}
  +
  \begin{pmatrix}\Svec_S\\[2pt]\Svec_F\end{pmatrix}\nion .
  \label{eq:partitioned}
\end{equation}

Section~\ref{sec:two_clocks} justifies this particular partition rather than
some other: the slow eigenvector is $1.0000$ ground state with every excited
component below $10^{-3}$, so the slow mode and the ground state are the same
object to the precision that matters.

Setting $\mathrm{d}\nF/\mathrm{d}t = 0$ in the lower block gives an algebraic
system for the excited populations,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\ \nF^{\mathrm{QSS}}
  = -\Lmat_{FF}^{-1}\left(\Lmat_{FS}\,\nS + \Svec_F\,\nion\right).\ }
  \label{eq:qss_solution}
\end{equation}

Read the right-hand side as a sentence. The bracket is everything arriving in
the excited manifold from outside it: $\Lmat_{FS}\nS$ is excitation up from the
ground state, $\Svec_F\nion$ is recombination down from the ions. The factor
$-\Lmat_{FF}^{-1}$ divides by the loss network. It is a matrix rather than a
number only because a level's losses feed other levels, which have their own
losses, and so on --- the division is over the whole network at once. For a
single Bodenstein intermediate it would collapse to $1/k_{\mathrm{loss}}$.

Two things make Eq.~\eqref{eq:qss_solution} usable. $\Lmat_{FF}$ is invertible
because it is a strictly column-diagonally-dominant matrix with negative
diagonal: every excited level loses population to the ground state or to the
continuum, so each column sum is strictly negative rather than zero, and the
Levy--Desplanques theorem applies. Physically, there are no perfectly trapped
states within the excited manifold. Its condition number lies between
$1.48\times10^{3}$ and $1.74\times10^{5}$ across the grid, so the inversion is
numerically well behaved despite the eight orders of magnitude spanned by the
entries.

\subsection{The reduced dynamics}

Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:qss_solution} back into the slow block eliminates
$\nF$ entirely:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathrm{d}\nS}{\mathrm{d}t}
  = \underbrace{\left(\Lmat_{SS} - \Lmat_{SF}\Lmat_{FF}^{-1}\Lmat_{FS}\right)}_{\Omqss}\nS
  + \left(\Svec_S - \Lmat_{SF}\Lmat_{FF}^{-1}\Svec_F\right)\nion .
  \label{eq:reduced}
\end{equation}

A 43-dimensional differential system has become a one-dimensional one. The
matrix $\Omqss$ is the \emph{Schur complement} of $\Lmat_{FF}$ in $\Lmat$, and
it has a direct physical reading: $\Lmat_{SS}$ is the direct loss from the
ground state, and $-\Lmat_{SF}\Lmat_{FF}^{-1}\Lmat_{FS}$ is the correction for
atoms that leave the ground state, wander through the excited manifold, and
either come back or are ionised on the way. It is stepwise ionisation, summed
over every possible path.

\textbf{This is the object the rest of the thesis is about.} $\Omqss$ is
precisely the effective ionisation rate that a plasma modeller using QSS would
construct, and it is the algebraic form of the effective coefficients tabulated
in frameworks such as ADAS~\cite{Summers2006}. When
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} asks what the approximation costs, it is asking what
this quantity costs.

\subsection{When is it valid?}

The elimination assumed the excited states are always at their instantaneous
steady value. The error in that assumption can be estimated directly. Write the
deviation from the instantaneous target as
$\bm{\delta}(t) = \nF(t) - \nF^{\mathrm{QSS}}(t)$ and subtract
Eq.~\eqref{eq:qss_solution} from the lower block of Eq.~\eqref{eq:partitioned}:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{\delta}}{\mathrm{d}t}
  = \Lmat_{FF}\bm{\delta}
  - \frac{\mathrm{d}\nF^{\mathrm{QSS}}}{\mathrm{d}t}.
  \label{eq:deviation}
\end{equation}

The deviation relaxes at the rate set by $\Lmat_{FF}$, whose slowest mode is
$\taurel$, and is forced by the motion of the target itself. If the driving
conditions change on a timescale $\taudrive$, then
$|\mathrm{d}\nF^{\mathrm{QSS}}/\mathrm{d}t| \sim
\|\nF^{\mathrm{QSS}}\|/\taudrive$, and balancing the two terms in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:deviation} at quasi-static equilibrium gives
$\|\bm{\delta}\| \sim \taurel\|\nF^{\mathrm{QSS}}\|/\taudrive$, that is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\|\nF - \nF^{\mathrm{QSS}}\|}{\|\nF^{\mathrm{QSS}}\|}
  \sim \frac{\taurel}{\taudrive},
  \label{eq:qss_validity}
\end{equation}

\noindent so the approximation is accurate whenever $\taudrive \gg \taurel$. For
the self-consistent problem, where the only thing driving the excited states is
the slow subsystem itself, $\taudrive \to \tauqss$ and the condition becomes
\begin{equation}
  \Msep = \frac{\tauqss}{\taurel} \gg 1 .
  \label{eq:qss_condition}
\end{equation}

Section~\ref{sec:two_clocks} measured $\Msep \ge 86.8$ at every grid point and
$\Msep = 9982$ at the benchmark. \textbf{The condition is satisfied everywhere,
comfortably.}

Two qualifications must travel with that statement.

\textbf{Equation~\eqref{eq:qss_validity} is an estimate, not a bound.} The step
from Eq.~\eqref{eq:deviation} to Eq.~\eqref{eq:qss_validity} identified the
relaxation rate of $\bm{\delta}$ with the slowest eigenvalue of $\Lmat_{FF}$.
Section~\ref{sec:two_clocks} warned that for a strongly non-normal operator the
spectrum does not control finite-time behaviour, and that warning applies here:
the balance in Eq.~\eqref{eq:deviation} is a \emph{quasi-static} argument, valid
once the fast transients have decayed and the deviation has settled onto the
slow manifold. It says nothing about what $\bm{\delta}$ does in the first few
$\taurel$ after a disturbance, when the non-orthogonality of the eigenvectors
can allow transient growth. Whether that matters for the quantity actually
measured is not decidable from the spectrum, and Chapter~\ref{ch:results}
settles it by integrating the full system rather than by argument.

\textbf{The condition is sufficient, not sharp.} At the benchmark point
Eq.~\eqref{eq:qss_validity} gives $\Msep^{-1} \approx 10^{-4}$, whereas direct
integration returns a residual of order $10^{-8}$: the approximation is four
orders of magnitude better than the scaling argument requires. The unwritten
prefactor --- set by how strongly the observable projects onto the moving part of
the target --- is evidently small. Equation~\eqref{eq:qss_validity} predicts the
trend correctly and the magnitude not at all, and it should be quoted only for
the former.

That would appear to end the matter. It does not, and the reason is the subject
of the next section.

\section{Two reference states}
\label{sec:two_references}

% GAP-CH1: the diagnostic chain -- why a line ratio is the measured quantity
\subsection{Why ratios}

A spectrometer viewing a divertor records a line-integrated brightness. Turning
that into an absolute population requires the neutral density along the
sightline and the path length, neither of which is known to better than a factor
of a few. Practitioners therefore work with \emph{ratios}, in which both cancel.

The ratio used throughout this thesis is
\begin{equation}
  \Robs \equiv \frac{n_3}{n_4}
  = \frac{n_{3s}+n_{3p}+n_{3d}}{n_{4s}+n_{4p}+n_{4d}+n_{4f}},
  \label{eq:observable_def}
\end{equation}

\noindent the population ratio of the $n=3$ and $n=4$ shells. Up to Einstein
coefficients it is the Balmer $H_\alpha/H_\beta$ ratio, since $H_\alpha$ is the
$3\to2$ transition and $H_\beta$ the $4\to2$;
Section~\ref{sec:balmer_equivalence} shows the two agree to
\SI{0.06}{\percent}. It is convenient to normalise to the ground state as well,
writing $\rp = n_p/\ngs$, since that is the form in which the QSS solution is
usually tabulated.

\subsection{The hidden variable}

Dividing Eq.~\eqref{eq:qss_solution} through by the ground-state population
gives the QSS ratios
\begin{equation}
  \rp^{\mathrm{QSS}}
  = \frac{\left[-\Lmat_{FF}^{-1}\left(\Lmat_{FS}\nS + \Svec_F\nion\right)\right]_p}
         {\ngs}.
  \label{eq:qss_ratio}
\end{equation}

It is tempting --- and, in the literature, common --- to treat
Eq.~\eqref{eq:qss_ratio} as a function of $\Te$ and $\nel$ alone, tabulate it,
and invert a measured ratio against the table. That step deserves more scrutiny
than it usually receives.

Look at what the numerator contains. The term $\Lmat_{FS}\nS$ is proportional to
the ground-state density; the term $\Svec_F\nion$ is proportional to the ion
density. What fixes $\rp^{\mathrm{QSS}}$ is not either supply on its own but the
\emph{ratio} of the two.

It is convenient to measure that ratio against its equilibrium value. Define the
ground-state \emph{departure coefficient}
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\ \bdep \equiv \frac{\ngs}{\Zsb{1}\,\nel\,\nion}\ }
  \label{eq:departure}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\Zsb{1}$ is the Saha--Boltzmann coefficient of the ground
state,
\begin{equation}
  \Zsb{p}\,[\si{\cubic\centi\metre}]
  = 10^{6}\,\frac{g_p}{2g_i}
  \left(\frac{h^2}{2\pi m_e\,e\,\Te}\right)^{3/2}
  \exp\!\left(\frac{\chi_p}{\Te}\right),
  \qquad \chi_p = \frac{13.606}{p^2},
  \label{eq:saha_boltzmann}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\Te$ and $\chi_p$ are both in \si{\electronvolt}, $e$ is the
elementary charge in \si{\coulomb} (converting \si{\electronvolt} to
\si{\joule}), the bracket evaluates in \si{\cubic\metre}, and the factor
$10^{6}$ converts to \si{\cubic\centi\metre}. Here $g_p = 2p^2$ is the
statistical weight of level $p$, $g_i = 1$ that of the bare proton, and the
factor $2$ in the denominator is the spin degeneracy of the free electron.
Temperatures are quoted in electron-volts throughout this thesis; no Boltzmann
constant appears, and $\Te$ should everywhere be read as the electron thermal
energy.

$\Zsb{p}$ is the factor for which $n_p = \Zsb{p}\nel\nion$ holds in complete
thermodynamic equilibrium, so $\bdep$ is dimensionless and equals unity there.
Departures above or below unity measure over- and under-population relative to
that thermodynamic reference; no more should be read into the sign than that.

It is worth attaching a number to $\bdep$ immediately, because the name
``departure coefficient'' suggests a small correction and it is nothing of the
kind. Evaluated on the collisional--radiative steady state, $\bdep$ runs from
$76$ in the hottest and densest corner of the grid to $2.7\times10^{5}$ in the
coldest and thinnest, and equals $956$ at the benchmark point. The ground
state is over-populated relative to Saha--Boltzmann by two to five orders of
magnitude throughout, which is simply the statement that an optically thin
plasma at these densities is far from thermodynamic equilibrium: radiative decay
returns atoms to the ground state faster than collisions can lift them out, and
the escaping photons are not replaced. A quantity that varies over three decades
across the operating range is not a small correction, and treating it as fixed
is the assumption this thesis examines.

\textbf{$\bdep$ is a dynamical variable}, and it is worth being precise about
how it evolves. Differentiating Eq.~\eqref{eq:departure} at fixed $\nion$,
\begin{equation}
  \dot{\bdep}
  = \frac{\dot{\ngs}}{\Zsb{1}\nel\nion}
  - \bdep\left(\frac{\dot{\Zsb{1}}}{\Zsb{1}}
                + \frac{\dot{\nel}}{\nel}\right).
  \label{eq:b1_dynamics}
\end{equation}

\noindent Only the first term comes from Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced}, which governs
$\ngs$; the second comes from the normalisation moving. This distinction matters
for the temperature-step experiment of Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. At the instant
of a step, $\ngs$ is continuous but $\Te$ is not, so $\Zsb{1}(\Te)$ jumps and
$\bdep$ changes discontinuously purely because its reference has changed. Once
$\Te$ and $\nel$ are again held fixed, $\bdep \propto \ngs$ and its subsequent
relaxation is that of the ground-state population, on $\tauqss$.

Either way, $\bdep(t)$ is not determined by the instantaneous $(\Te, \nel)$ ---
it carries the plasma's history. Nothing in the QSS derivation fixes it.

\subsection{Does the observable actually depend on it?}
\label{sec:R_depends_on_b}

The previous subsection shows that each \emph{individual} normalised population
depends on $\bdep$. That is not yet an argument about $\Robs$. A sceptical
reader is entitled to object: perhaps every level depends on $\bdep$ and the
dependence cancels in the ratio, leaving $H_\alpha/H_\beta$ a clean function of
$(\Te,\nel)$ after all. Ratios are chosen precisely because things cancel in
them. The objection must be answered, not assumed away.

It can be answered exactly, because Eq.~\eqref{eq:qss_solution} is linear in the
two supplies. Define
\begin{equation}
  \bm{a} \equiv -\Lmat_{FF}^{-1}\Lmat_{FS},
  \qquad
  \bm{c} \equiv -\Lmat_{FF}^{-1}\Svec_F ,
  \label{eq:ac_def}
\end{equation}

\noindent so that Eq.~\eqref{eq:qss_solution} reads
\begin{equation}
  \nF^{\mathrm{QSS}} = \bm{a}\,\ngs + \bm{c}\,\nion .
  \label{eq:two_supplies}
\end{equation}

Both vectors are dimensionless and both have a plain reading. $a_p$ is the
number of atoms that end up sitting in level $p$ per ground-state atom present:
not only those excited into $p$ directly, but those excited anywhere and
arriving at $p$ afterwards by cascade or by a further collision. The matrix
inverse is what performs that sum over every possible route. Likewise $c_p$ is
the population of $p$ per ion, from electrons captured into any level and
settling into $p$ on the way down. Neither depends on how many ground-state
atoms or ions are actually present --- both are properties of $\Lmat_{FF}$,
$\Lmat_{FS}$ and $\Svec_F$ alone, and therefore functions of $(\Te,\nel)$ only.

So Eq.~\eqref{eq:two_supplies} says something simple: every excited level is
fed from two directions, and its population is the sum of two terms, one
proportional to the size of the reservoir below it and one to the reservoir
above. What distinguishes one level from another is the \emph{proportion} in
which it draws on the two.

Writing $a_m$ and $c_m$ for the sums over the $\ell$-resolved states of shell
$m$, and using $\ngs = \bdep\Zsb{1}\nel\nion$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:departure},
\begin{equation}
  n_m^{\mathrm{QSS}}
  = \nion\left[a_m\,\bdep\,\Zsb{1}\,\nel + c_m\right].
  \label{eq:shell_population}
\end{equation}

\noindent The prefactor $\nion$ is common to both shells and cancels in the
ratio, as does the path length and the neutral density. What does not cancel is
the bracket:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\
  \Rqss(\bdep) = \frac{a_3\,\bdep\,\Zsb{1}\,\nel + c_3}
                      {a_4\,\bdep\,\Zsb{1}\,\nel + c_4}\ }
  \label{eq:R_of_b}
\end{equation}

Differentiating,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\
  \frac{\partial \Rqss}{\partial \bdep}
  = \frac{\Zsb{1}\,\nel\,\left(a_3c_4 - a_4c_3\right)}
         {\left(a_4\,\bdep\,\Zsb{1}\,\nel + c_4\right)^2}\ }
  \label{eq:dR_db}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{fig3_2_R_of_b1.pdf}
  \caption[The observable as a function of the hidden variable]{%
  The shell ratio $\Rqss$ as a function of the ground-state departure
  coefficient, at the benchmark point, from Eq.~\eqref{eq:R_of_b}. The curve is
  a one-parameter family: it asks what $\Robs$ would be if $\bdep$ took other
  values at fixed $(\Te,\nel)$. Between the two supply limits the observable
  changes by a factor of several; a table indexed on $(\Te,\nel)$ alone must
  select one point on this curve. The marker shows the value the
  CR-equilibrium closure selects, and the shaded strip the range of
  $\bdep^{\mathrm{CRE}}$ over the whole grid.}
  \label{fig:R_of_b1}
\end{figure}

The observable is independent of $\bdep$ if and only if
\begin{equation}
  a_3c_4 = a_4c_3
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \frac{a_3}{c_3} = \frac{a_4}{c_4},
  \label{eq:cancellation_condition}
\end{equation}

\noindent that is, if and only if the $n=3$ and $n=4$ shells draw on the two
supplies in \emph{identical proportion}. There is no reason for them to. The
$n=4$ shell lies closer to the continuum and is more readily fed by
recombination; the $n=3$ shell is more readily reached by excitation from the
ground state. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} measures the difference and finds it
substantial across most of the operating range.

\subsection{The ground-fed fraction, and where the sensitivity vanishes}
\label{sec:ground_fed_fraction}

Equation~\eqref{eq:dR_db} settles whether the dependence exists. It is not,
however, the right measure of how \emph{strong} it is, because $\Robs$ itself
varies over orders of magnitude across the operating range and an absolute
derivative is not comparable between grid points. The dimensionless measure is
the logarithmic sensitivity, and it takes a form with a direct physical reading.

Define the \emph{ground-fed fraction} of shell $m$,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\ \ffrac{m} \equiv
  \frac{a_m\,\bdep\,\Zsb{1}\,\nel}
        {a_m\,\bdep\,\Zsb{1}\,\nel + c_m}\ }
  \label{eq:ground_fed_fraction}
\end{equation}

\noindent the share of shell $m$'s population that arrived from below, by
excitation out of the ground state, as opposed to from above by recombination.
That $\ffrac{m}$ lies in $[0,1]$ is not automatic: it requires $a_m \ge 0$ and
$c_m \ge 0$, and both are components of $-\Lmat_{FF}^{-1}$ acting on non-negative
vectors. The claim therefore needs $-\Lmat_{FF}^{-1}$ to be non-negative
\emph{elementwise}, which holds for the same reason $\Lmat_{FF}$ is invertible
(Section~\ref{sec:qss}): $-\Lmat_{FF}$ has non-positive off-diagonal entries and
strictly positive column sums, making it a non-singular M-matrix, and the
inverse of a non-singular M-matrix is elementwise non-negative. Physically the
statement is unsurprising --- adding atoms to one level cannot reduce the steady
population of another --- but it is worth recording, because the two limits below
depend on it. Direct evaluation confirms it: at none of the 400 grid points does
$-\Lmat_{FF}^{-1}$ contain a negative entry.

Differentiating
Eq.~\eqref{eq:R_of_b} logarithmically then gives
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\ \frac{\mathrm{d}\ln\Robs}{\mathrm{d}\ln\bdep}
  = \ffrac{3} - \ffrac{4}\ }
  \label{eq:log_sensitivity}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{fig3_3_ground_fed.pdf}
  \caption[Ground-fed fractions and the sensitivity of the observable]{%
  The ground-fed fractions of the two shells and their difference, at the
  benchmark point. Both fractions rise from zero to unity as the ground-state
  reservoir grows, but they do so at different rates, and their difference ---
  which by Eq.~\eqref{eq:log_sensitivity} is exactly
  $\mathrm{d}\ln\Robs/\mathrm{d}\ln\bdep$ --- vanishes in both limits and
  peaks where the two supplies compete. Note that $\ffrac{3}-\ffrac{4}$ is a
  difference of fractions, not itself a fraction.}
  \label{fig:ground_fed}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The Balmer ratio's sensitivity to the ground-state reservoir is exactly
the difference in ground-fed fraction between the two shells.} This is the
mathematical core of the thesis, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:log_sensitivity} is where the
physics becomes visible.

It vanishes in two limits, and only there. When $\bdep\Zsb{1}\nel \gg
c_m/a_m$ for both shells, the plasma is strongly ionising: both shells are fed
almost entirely from the ground state, $\ffrac{3} = \ffrac{4} = 1$, and the
ratio is blind to the reservoir. When $\bdep\Zsb{1}\nel \ll c_m/a_m$, the
plasma is strongly recombining, $\ffrac{3} = \ffrac{4} = 0$, and the ratio is
blind again. \emph{In between --- where the two supplies compete --- the shells
are fed in different proportion and the sensitivity is largest.}

Note that this is the logarithmic sensitivity, not the absolute derivative
$\partial\Robs/\partial\bdep$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:dR_db}. The latter does
\emph{not} vanish as $\bdep \to 0$; it tends to
$\Zsb{1}\nel(a_3c_4-a_4c_3)/c_4^2$, which is finite. The two limits are
statements about the \emph{fractional} response of the observable, which is what
a diagnostic error is.

The classification into ionising and recombining plasmas is due to
Fujimoto~\cite{Fujimoto2004}, and the crossover between the two is what a
divertor passes through as it detaches.
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} maps $\ffrac{3} - \ffrac{4}$ over the operating range
and shows the diagnostic error tracking it.

It is worth recording what Eq.~\eqref{eq:R_of_b} is not. It is not a fit, an
expansion, or an approximation: it is an exact rewriting of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:qss_solution}, valid wherever $\Lmat_{FF}$ is invertible.
Section~\ref{sec:fujimoto} verifies the decomposition numerically against
published population coefficients, and Chapter~\ref{ch:results} verifies the
superposition $\nF = \bm{a}\ngs + \bm{c}\nion$ to a relative error of
$3\times10^{-14}$.

\subsection{Two reference states, and they are not the same}

Equation~\eqref{eq:R_of_b} shows that the QSS ratio is a function of three
arguments, not two. This gives two distinct things a measurement can be compared
against.

\paragraph{The QSS reference.} $\Rqss(\Te, \nel;\,\bdep(t))$, evaluated at the
\emph{instantaneous} departure coefficient. This is what adiabatic elimination
claims: the excited manifold is in steady state with whatever reservoir is
present at that moment. Its accuracy is governed by
Section~\ref{sec:qss}'s validity condition.

\paragraph{The CR-equilibrium reference.}
\begin{equation}
  \Rcre(\Te, \nel)
  \equiv \Rqss\!\left(\Te, \nel;\,\bdep^{\mathrm{CRE}}(\Te,\nel)\right),
  \label{eq:cre_ratio}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\bdep^{\mathrm{CRE}}$ is obtained by additionally setting
$\mathrm{d}\nS/\mathrm{d}t = 0$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:reduced} and solving for the
steady ground-state population. This \emph{is} a function of $(\Te,\nel)$ alone
and can therefore be tabulated.

The distinction is easy to miss because both are called ``the QSS solution'' in
common usage, and in a steady plasma they are the same number. They separate
whenever the ground-state population has not finished settling --- which, in a
divertor disturbed by an edge-localised mode every few milliseconds, is most of
the time.

Two remarks on terminology, both of which matter for what follows. First,
$\bdep = 1$ denotes complete thermodynamic equilibrium, whereas
$\bdep^{\mathrm{CRE}}$ denotes the steady state of the collisional--radiative
system, which is a different and much weaker condition; in an optically thin
ionising plasma $\bdep^{\mathrm{CRE}}$ is very much larger than unity. Second,
this model holds the ion density fixed (Section~\ref{sec:rate_equation}), so
the quantity relaxing on $\tauqss$ is the ground-state population against a
prescribed reservoir, not a self-consistent ionisation balance. What that
restriction costs is assessed in Chapter~\ref{ch:limits}.

\subsection{Two error measures}

Each reference defines an error:
\begin{align}
  \epsQSS(t) &= \left|\frac{\Robs(t)}
                     {\Rqss\!\left(\Te,\nel;\,\bdep(t)\right)} - 1\right|,
  \label{eq:eps_qss}\\[4pt]
  \epsCRE(t) &= \left|\frac{\Robs(t)}
                     {\Rcre\!\left(\Te,\nel\right)} - 1\right|.
  \label{eq:eps_cre}
\end{align}

$\epsQSS$ asks whether the excited states have kept up with the reservoir they
actually see. $\epsCRE$ asks whether the tabulated answer is right. The first is
the question the literature worries about; the second is the one a
diagnostician suffers.

\textbf{They are not the same question, and Chapter~\ref{ch:results} shows they
have very different answers.} Naming them apart is the single most important
definitional step in this thesis: conflating them makes it possible to measure
the second and report it as the first.

\subsection{What a two-parameter table must assume}

It remains to say precisely what is being assumed when a measured ratio is
inverted against a table indexed on $(\Te,\nel)$.

Equation~\eqref{eq:R_of_b} establishes that $\Robs = \Robs(\Te,\nel,\bdep)$.
A two-parameter table must therefore do one of three things: adopt a closure
$\bdep = \bdep(\Te,\nel)$; operate in a regime where one supply dominates both
shells, so that Eq.~\eqref{eq:dR_db} is negligible and the third argument does
not matter; or hold some other quantity fixed that determines $\bdep$
implicitly. Equilibrium ionisation balance is a closure, not a mathematical
necessity, and it is important not to overstate the point.

It is important to be precise about which practice is being criticised, because
the modern effective-coefficient databases are not themselves at fault.
ADAS~\cite{Summers2006}, for instance, tabulates photon emissivity coefficients
separately for the excitation-driven and recombination-driven components. Used
as intended, those two components are weighted by $\ngs$ and $\nion$
respectively, which is exactly the information $\bdep$ carries --- the database
does not impose a closure at all. Indeed the excitation/recombination split of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:two_supplies} is conceptually the same decomposition.

The assumption enters at the \emph{inversion} step, when a measured ratio is
mapped to $(\Te,\nel)$ through a two-parameter lookup. Such a lookup returns a
single ratio per $(\Te,\nel)$ pair and therefore requires the two components to
have been combined in a fixed proportion --- that is, it requires a closure for
$\bdep$. The usual choice is $\bdep = \bdep^{\mathrm{CRE}}$, obtained by solving
the collisional--radiative system to complete steady state. A ratio inverted this
way carries the assumption that the plasma's ground-state population has settled
at its steady value for the local conditions: not an assumption about how quickly
excited states equilibrate, but an assumption that the plasma has \emph{finished
changing}.

The second possibility is not merely hypothetical, and it is the reason
Eq.~\eqref{eq:dR_db} is worth having. Where one supply dominates both shells,
the table is safe regardless of what the ground state is doing.
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} maps where in the divertor operating range that is the
case, and where it is not.

The remainder of this thesis quantifies what the assumption costs.

\section{A caveat: the modes are not orthogonal}
\label{sec:transient_growth}

Section~\ref{sec:two_clocks} presented the eigen-decomposition as a change of
coordinates in which the 43 coupled equations become 43 independent decays. That
is exact. But a change of coordinates is only as convenient as the coordinates
are well behaved, and these are not.

The reason is a physical asymmetry running through the whole matrix. Radiative
decay carries atoms downward at up to $\SI{6.3e8}{\per\second}$; electron-impact
excitation carries them upward at $C\nel \sim \SI{e4}{\per\second}$ at the
benchmark density. Downward and upward coupling therefore differ by some nine
orders of magnitude, and $\Lmat$ is very far from symmetric. Its eigenvectors,
consequently, are very far from orthogonal.

The consequence is worth stating carefully, because it is easy to overstate.
Every eigenvalue has negative real part, so every mode decays and any
perturbation eventually vanishes; that much the spectrum does guarantee.
What it does not guarantee is that the perturbation decays \emph{monotonically}.
When eigenvectors are nearly parallel, a state of modest size can be the sum of
two much larger, nearly cancelling modal contributions. If those two modes decay
at different rates the cancellation is progressively spoiled, and the state can
grow --- sometimes by orders of magnitude --- before the decay of the individual
modes finally wins. The standard diagnostic for whether this is possible at all
is the numerical abscissa
\begin{equation}
  \mu(\Lmat) = \lambda_{\max}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\left[\Lmat + \Lmat^{\mathsf T}\right]\right),
  \label{eq:numerical_abscissa}
\end{equation}

\noindent the largest eigenvalue of the symmetric part of $\Lmat$, which gives
the initial slope of $\|\nvec(t)\|$ for the worst-case perturbation. For this
matrix $\mu(\Lmat) > 0$ at every grid point, so transient growth is not merely
possible but guaranteed for \emph{some} disturbance.

Whether it occurs for the disturbance and the observable that matter here is a
different question, and one the spectrum cannot answer. $\mu(\Lmat) > 0$ is a
statement about the worst case over all perturbations and all norms; the Balmer
ratio is one particular scalar function of the state, and a temperature step is
one particular disturbance. A quantity may be entirely well behaved even when
the operator that governs it admits growth in principle. This is why
Section~\ref{sec:qss} was careful to label Eq.~\eqref{eq:qss_validity} an
estimate rather than a bound: the eigenvalue argument describes the system after
the transients have settled, and the transients themselves are outside its
reach.

Chapter~\ref{ch:results} therefore settles the question the only way it can be
settled --- by integrating the full 43-state system through a temperature step
and measuring what the Balmer ratio does, rather than by inferring it from the
spectrum.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:theory_summary}

Starting from a population balance on each of 43 levels, this chapter assembled
the collisional--radiative rate equation $\dot{\nvec} = \Lmat\nvec + \Svec\nion$
and checked the assembly against particle conservation, which holds to
$3.6\times10^{-11}$ across the operating range.

Diagonalising $\Lmat$ revealed a spectrum with one gap and then a continuum: a
single slow mode at $\tauqss = \SI{22.7}{\micro\second}$, the relaxation of the
ground-state population, sitting four orders of magnitude above a dense band of
fast modes whose slowest member is $\taurel = \SI{2.28}{\nano\second}$, the
rearrangement of the excited manifold. The separation
$\Msep = \tauqss/\taurel$ is 9982 at the benchmark point and never falls below
$86.8$ anywhere on the grid.

That separation licenses the quasi-steady-state approximation: eliminate the
excited populations algebraically and solve only for the slow subsystem. The
elimination is the Bodenstein steady-state approximation applied to 42 coupled
intermediates at once, and it produces the Schur complement $\Omqss$ --- the
effective ionisation rate that a plasma modeller using QSS constructs, and the
algebraic form of the effective coefficients that spectroscopic inversions rest
on. Its validity condition, $\Msep \gg 1$, is satisfied everywhere and
comfortably.

The chapter's central result is that this is not the end of the argument. The
QSS ratio
\begin{equation*}
  \Rqss(\bdep) = \frac{a_3\,\bdep\,\Zsb{1}\,\nel + c_3}
                      {a_4\,\bdep\,\Zsb{1}\,\nel + c_4}
\end{equation*}

\noindent is a function of \emph{three} arguments, not two. The third is the
ground-state departure coefficient $\bdep$, which is dynamical: it carries the
plasma's history and takes a time $\tauqss$ to settle. Its logarithmic
sensitivity is exactly
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{\mathrm{d}\ln\Robs}{\mathrm{d}\ln\bdep} = \ffrac{3} - \ffrac{4},
\end{equation*}

\noindent the difference in ground-fed fraction between the two shells, which
vanishes when either supply dominates both shells and is largest where the two
compete. A table indexed on $(\Te,\nel)$ alone must therefore adopt a closure for
$\bdep$, and the closure adopted in practice is complete collisional--radiative
equilibrium.

Two error measures follow and must not be conflated. $\epsQSS$ asks whether the
excited states have kept up with the reservoir they actually see; $\epsCRE$ asks
whether the tabulated answer is right. The first is the question the literature
worries about. The second is the one a diagnostician suffers.

Everything in this chapter has been an argument about equations. Whether the
equations describe hydrogen is a separate question, and Chapter~\ref{ch:validation}
addresses it: what would a wrong model fail, and does this one fail it?
