\chapter{What the Model Says}
\label{ch:results}

Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} left the argument at a definition and a worry. The
definition: a Balmer-ratio lookup table indexed on $(\Te,\nel)$ is a table in
\emph{three} arguments with the third one hidden, and the hidden argument is
the ground-state departure coefficient $\bdep$, which takes a time $\tauslow$ to
settle after the plasma changes. The worry, which is the one the literature
carries: the excited states are what a spectrometer sees, and they may not keep
up with a plasma that changes on microseconds. Chapter~\ref{ch:validation}
established that the rate matrix these questions are asked of reproduces
independent atomic data where such data exist, and reported the two errors that
an audit of this work found and corrected.

This chapter evaluates both errors over the operating range, and the two
results are worth stating before the evidence rather than after it.

The first is a reversal. The quasi-steady-state (QSS) closure, the
approximation that the excited populations sit in instantaneous steady state
with whatever ground-state reservoir is present, is not the problem. Measured
along a full time-dependent trajectory it is accurate to eight significant
figures. What fails is the assumption that the reservoir itself has finished
moving, which is a different assumption, made at a different step of the
inversion, and never previously separated from the first.

The second is that what that failure costs is not a number. It is a function,
and the function has a closed form,
%
\begin{equation*}
  \epsplat
  = \Big|\exp\!\big(\overline{S}\;G\;\Delta\ln\Te\big) - 1\Big| ,
\end{equation*}
%
in which $\Delta\ln\Te$ is the size of the transient the reader cares about,
$G$ measures how far the ground-state reservoir is dragged by it, and
$\overline{S}$ measures how strongly the line ratio answers to the reservoir.
The last of these is bounded above by a quantity that no choice of atomic data
can push past unity. So the chapter reports the structure and the bound, and
lets a reader compute the error for their own transient. A single percentage
would be an answer to a question about the grid this thesis happens to use, and
Section~\ref{sec:step_convention} shows how badly that goes.

Both results are stated over $\Te\ge\SI{2}{\electronvolt}$.
Section~\ref{sec:scope} shows that this is not a hedge: it is the region of the
operating grid on which the model's own assumptions are mutually consistent, and
two unrelated pieces of physics place the boundary in the same place.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{What this chapter takes as given.} From
Chapter~\ref{ch:theory}: the rate equation
$\dot{\nvec} = \Lmat\nvec + \Svec\nion$ and its two timescales
$\taurel$ and $\tauslow$; the QSS elimination and its Schur complement; the two
reference states $\Rqss$ and $\Rcre$ and the two error measures
$\epsQSS$ and $\epsCRE$ built on them; the two-supply decomposition
$\nF = \bm{a}\,\ngs + \bm{c}\,\nion$; the ground-fed fraction $\ffrac{m}$, its
logistic form, and the identity
$\mathrm{d}\ln\Robs/\mathrm{d}\ln\bdep = \ffrac{3}-\ffrac{4}$. From
Chapter~\ref{ch:intro}: the operating range
$\Te = 1$--\SI{10}{\electronvolt}, $\nel =
10^{12}$--\SI{e15}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}, and the fact that an edge-localised
mode (ELM) disturbs a divertor for of order \SI{100}{\micro\second}. That
duration is the aggressive end of the range; the value extrapolated to the ITER
pedestal is \SI{506}{\micro\second} \cite[Sec.~5]{Loarte2003}, and
Section~\ref{sec:persistence} carries both.
The benchmark point $\Te = \benchTe$, $\nel = \benchne$, grid index $[23,5]$,
runs through this chapter as it runs through the last.

\section{What happens after a temperature step}
\label{sec:step_response}
% QUESTION: when the electron temperature changes abruptly, what does the
% 43-level population vector actually do, and on what timescales?
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Take a plasma sitting quietly at collisional--radiative equilibrium (CRE), the
steady state of the full 43-level system, and change the electron temperature
abruptly at $t=0$, holding $\nel$ fixed. Three things then happen, on three
separated timescales, and the middle one is where this thesis lives.

For the first few nanoseconds the excited manifold rearranges. Every one of the
42 excited levels is a reactive intermediate in the chemical-engineering sense:
it holds almost no population, it is created and destroyed thousands of times
faster than the species feeding it changes, and it therefore reaches a
steady state with respect to that species almost immediately. At the benchmark
point this takes $\taurel = \SI{2.28}{\nano\second}$.

For the next several microseconds nothing further appears to happen, and this
is the interesting part. The excited populations have settled, but they have
settled onto the \emph{old} ground-state population, because the ground state
holds essentially all the atoms and cannot turn over in nanoseconds. The system
occupies a state that is steady in its fast variables and stale in its slow
one. Call it \emph{partial equilibrium} (PE). At the benchmark point it lasts
until the ground state has moved, on $\tauslow = \SI{22.7}{\micro\second}$, so
the plateau is some four orders of magnitude wider than the relaxation that
built it.

Only then does the plasma reach the new collisional--radiative equilibrium that
a lookup table would have assigned to it the instant the temperature changed.
Figure~\ref{fig:trajectory} shows all three phases on a single logarithmic time
axis, at the benchmark and at the coldest grid point.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig5_6_trajectory}
  \caption{\CapFigTrajectory}
  \label{fig:trajectory}
\end{figure}

The ELM that motivates this thesis lasts of order \SI{100}{\micro\second},
which is the aggressive end of the range: the duration extrapolated to the ITER
pedestal is \SI{506}{\micro\second} \cite[Sec.~5]{Loarte2003}, and
Section~\ref{sec:persistence} reports both. The shorter figure
is long against $\taurel$ by a factor of about $4\times10^{4}$ and comparable
to $\tauslow$, so a spectrometer pointed at the divertor during one spends its
integration time looking at the plateau. That is the state whose error this
chapter measures.

\subsection{Two error measures, and why the step size is part of each}
\label{sec:eps_definitions}

Section~\ref{sec:two_references} fixed the two reference states against which an
observable can be judged. Two further quantities are needed here, and both are
properties of a temperature \emph{step} rather than of a single operator, which
is why neither appears in Chapter~\ref{ch:theory}.

Let the plasma sit at the collisional--radiative equilibrium of
$\Lmat(\Te,\nel)$, and let the electron temperature step to $\Te+\dTe$ at
$t=0$ with $\nel$ held fixed. Write $\Lmat^-$ and $\Lmat^+$ for the operators
before and after, in the notation of Section~\ref{sec:two_clocks}. The
\emph{step error}
\begin{equation}
  \epsstep = \left|\frac{\Rcre(\Te,\nel)}
                        {\Rcre(\Te+\dTe,\nel)} - 1\right|
  \label{eq:eps_step_def}
\end{equation}

\noindent is the dimensionless distance between the two tabulated answers: what
the reading would change by if the plasma re-equilibrated instantly. The
\emph{plateau error}
\begin{equation}
  \epsplat = \left|\frac{\Rpe}
                        {\Rcre(\Te+\dTe,\nel)} - 1\right|,
  \qquad
  \Rpe \equiv \Rqss\!\left(\Te+\dTe,\nel;\,u^-\right),
  \label{eq:eps_plateau_def}
\end{equation}

\noindent is the distance between the tabulated answer and the state the plasma
occupies once the excited manifold has settled but before the ground state has
moved: the post-step excited response of Eq.~\eqref{eq:qss_ratio}, evaluated on
the \emph{pre-step} reservoir.

\textbf{The third argument is $u^- = \ngs^-/\nion$ and not $\bdep^-$, and the
difference is a factor of $24$.} What is frozen across the step is the
ground-state \emph{population}, which is what transport and ionisation would
have to change. The departure coefficient is not frozen with it, because
$\bdep = u/(\Zsb{1}(\Te)\nel)$ carries the Saha coefficient and $\Zsb{1}$ moves
when $\Te$ does. Holding $\bdep^-$ instead of $u^-$ at the benchmark gives
$\epsplat = 0.00267$ rather than $0.0636$, because $\bdep^{\mathrm{CRE}}$ shifts
by only \SI{1.3}{\percent} across one interval, from $956.5$ to $968.9$, while
$u$ shifts by \SI{23.6}{\percent}. An earlier draft wrote $\bdep^-$ here; the
code has always frozen the population
(\texttt{verify\_reservoir\_gain.py}, line 143), so no computed number was
affected, but the definition as printed did not support the error the chapter
reports. Both are dimensionless, both are non-negative,
and both are zero for a zero step.

Keeping them separate matters because they are not close. Across the operating
grid $\epsplat$ exceeds $\epsstep$ at every one of the 680 point-and-direction
pairs that possess a plateau, with a median ratio near $12$ and a minimum of
$1.45$ (\texttt{verify\_plateau\_gridmap.py}). A diagnostician who pictures the
reading sliding smoothly from one tabulated value to the other has not merely
misjudged the size of the excursion; they have the wrong shape of transient.

That comparison carries a caution about the ratio itself. Because
$\log(\epsplat/\epsstep) = \log\epsplat - \log\epsstep$ identically, any
correlation between the amplification and $\epsstep$ is partly tautological,
and the amplification blows up wherever $\epsstep$ passes through zero. The
apparent grid maximum of $1271\times$ sits at the point of smallest $\epsstep$
on the grid, where $\epsplat$ is itself below the median
(\texttt{verify\_plateau\_gridmap.py}). $\epsplat$ is the primary quantity
throughout this chapter and the amplification is reported only as a
distribution.

\textbf{Both quantities are defined by a step, so neither has a value until the
step is named.} $\Lmat$ is tabulated only on the temperature grid, so every
step used here is an integer number of grid intervals, and one interval is a
factor $1.048113$, that is $+\SI{4.81}{\percent}$ heating or
$-\SI{4.59}{\percent}$ cooling
(\texttt{validation/plateau\_gridmap/plateau\_gridmap.txt}, lines 10--11 and
18). The reader should read $\SI{4.81}{\percent}$ as an artifact of the choice
of 50 logarithmically spaced points between $1$ and \SI{10}{\electronvolt}, and
nothing else. Every $\epsplat$ quoted in this chapter carries the step that
produced it.

\subsection{The closure everyone worries about is not the dominant error}
\label{sec:qss_is_exact}

Before the maps, the negative result that reframes them.

$\epsQSS$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_qss} asks whether the excited states have kept up
with the reservoir they actually see. Integrating the full 43-state system
through the step and evaluating both errors along the same trajectory gives, at
the benchmark point, $\epsQSS = 8.66\times10^{-6}$ against
$\epsCRE = 6.34\times10^{-2}$; at $[0,4]$, the grid point carrying the largest
$\epsCRE$ anywhere, $\epsQSS = 6.73\times10^{-9}$ against
$\epsCRE = 3.869\times10^{-1}$ (\texttt{findings\_09}~\S1, from direct
integration).

At those two points the two errors differ by 3.9 and 7.8 decades, and they
differ most at the point where the diagnostic error is worst. Over the 392
analysed cells the gap runs from 2.9 to 9.7 decades
(Figure~\ref{fig:reversal}b), so it is a distribution rather than a single
number and should not be quoted as one. Neither figure is zero: the
excited-state closure is an approximation and it carries a measurable residual.
The point is its size relative to the other error, not its vanishing. The adiabatic elimination of
Section~\ref{sec:qss} is doing its job to a part in $10^{8}$ precisely where the
tabulated answer is off by \SI{39}{\percent} per $+\SI{4.81}{\percent}$
temperature step.

The reason is the spectrum. Section~\ref{sec:two_clocks} found one slow mode
and then a band, with nothing in between: at $\Lmat[1,4]$, the post-step
operator at the worst point, the three slowest eigenvalues are
$-4.29$, $-1.60\times10^{8}$ and $-3.44\times10^{8}\,\si{\per\second}$, a gap of
$3.7\times10^{7}$ (\texttt{findings\_10}~\S3.2). An excited population cannot
lag a reservoir that moves $10^{7}$ times more slowly than it can respond.

Figure~\ref{fig:reversal} puts both errors on the same axes over the whole
grid. The closure residual never exceeds $4.4\times10^{-5}$ anywhere on the
analysed grid; the distance to the tabulated equilibrium runs from
\SI{1.66}{\percent} to \SI{38.7}{\percent} over the same cells.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig5_5_reversal}
  \caption{\CapFigReversal}
  \label{fig:reversal}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The failure is therefore not in the elimination but in the closure
chosen for the eliminated variable.} A two-parameter table sets
$\bdep = \bdep^{\mathrm{CRE}}(\Te,\nel)$, which asserts that the plasma has
finished changing. The rest of this chapter is about what that assertion costs.

\section{The error maps}
\label{sec:error_maps}

Figure~\ref{fig:eps_map} maps $\epsplat$ over the operating grid for a heating
step of one grid interval, $+\SI{4.81}{\percent}$ in $\Te$ at fixed $\nel$.
The grid is 50 temperatures by 8 densities, and each cell was evaluated by an
exact linear solve rather than by a fit: the superposition
$\nF = \bm{a}\,\ngs + \bm{c}\,\nion$ on which
Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_plateau_def} rests holds to a relative residual of
$3.075\times10^{-14}$ over the whole grid
(\texttt{plateau\_gridmap.txt}, line 19).

Three numbers anchor the map, and each is stated with the set it is an extremum
over, because this project has twice been caught quoting a
regime-restricted extremum globally.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Over all 680 analysed pairs, the largest value is
    $\epsplat = \SI{38.69}{\percent}$ per $+\SI{4.81}{\percent}$ step, at
    $[0,4]$: $\Te = \SI{1.000}{\electronvolt}$,
    $\nel = \SI{5.18e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$. It sits on the cold edge of
    the grid, so the temperature range is truncated there and the script that
    computes it says so in its own output
    (\texttt{plateau\_gridmap.txt}, lines 41--43: \texttt{sits on a grid EDGE:
    True}). Section~\ref{sec:scope} shows that this value is not quantitative
    and that the point is not a divertor state.
  \item Over the range this thesis defends, $\Te \ge \SI{2}{\electronvolt}$,
    the largest value is $\epsplat = \SI{18.07}{\percent}$ per
    $+\SI{4.81}{\percent}$ step, at $[15,3]$:
    $\Te = \SI{2.02}{\electronvolt}$,
    $\nel = \SI{1.93e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$
    (\texttt{verify\_eps\_gridmap.py}).
  \item At the benchmark point $[23,5]$, $\epsplat = \SI{6.36}{\percent}$ per
    $+\SI{4.81}{\percent}$ step (\texttt{verify\_eps\_gridmap.py}, and
    \texttt{verify\_plateau\_gridmap.py} to five digits: $0.063612$).
\end{itemize}

Cooling is not the mirror image of heating, and the asymmetry is mostly a step
artifact rather than physics: one grid interval down is
$-\SI{4.59}{\percent}$ rather than $+\SI{4.81}{\percent}$, and once the two are
matched per grid point and normalised, the median heat/cool ratio is $1.036$
(\texttt{thesis\_ready}~A8). What does not normalise away is the location of
the maximum, which moves from $[0,4]$ under heating to $[1,3]$ under cooling,
where $\epsplat = \SI{28.58}{\percent}$ and the point is no longer on a grid
edge (\texttt{plateau\_gridmap.txt}, lines 59--61).

\subsection{The density structure is a broad crest, not a line}

Figure~\ref{fig:eps_vs_ne} cuts the map along the density axis. Every
temperature row has a single interior maximum in $\nel$: the error is small at
both ends of the density range and largest in between.
Section~\ref{sec:density_mechanism} explains why. What matters first is how
sharply that maximum is located, because the temptation is to read a ridge line
off a colour map and quote a density.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{fig5_2_eps_vs_ne}
  \caption[The density dependence, all eight columns]{\CapFigEpsVsNe}
  \label{fig:eps_vs_ne}
\end{figure}

It is not sharply located. Across the peak column and its two neighbours, a
factor $7.2$ in density, $\epsplat$ varies by only $6$ to
\SI{22}{\percent} depending on the row, while over the full three decades the
same row falls by a factor $2.2$ to $6.6$
(\texttt{make\_ch5\_figures.py}, injected into the caption of
Figure~\ref{fig:eps_vs_ne}). The crest is real and it is wide-topped. The grid
spacing is a factor $2.683$ per column, so nothing finer than that is resolved
in any case.

\textbf{This chapter therefore quotes the density of maximum error as the range
$7\times10^{12}$ to $\SI{5e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$}, which is the pair of
grid columns bracketing the modal maximum at
$\SI{1.93e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, and not as a single value. Two further
facts support the range and neither supports a point value. The column at
$\SI{5.18e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ carries the row maximum in 3 of the 49
heating rows, all of them the coldest, and
$\SI{1.93e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ carries it in the rest; per-row argmax
over rows $0$ to $15$ is $[4,4,4,3,3,3,3,3,3,3,3,3,3,3,3,3]$
(\texttt{findings\_10}~\S1.2). And a parabolic fit in $\ln\nel$ through the
three columns around the maximum places its vertex at
$\SI{1.65e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, a \SI{15}{\percent} shift off the grid
node. That fit is now performed on every row
(\texttt{verify\_crest\_subgrid.py}, stamped to
\texttt{validation/crest\_subgrid/crest\_vertices.csv}), and the result needs
two qualifications the single number does not carry. Over the $49$ rows with an
interior maximum the vertex runs from $\SI{1.45e13}{}$ to
\SI{3.71e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre} with median
\SI{1.62e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}, so $\SI{1.65e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$
is the median rather than the value, and the row-to-row spread is a factor
$2.6$. And a parabola through three points is an interpolation with no residual
to report: refitting through five points where five exist moves the vertex by
between $-3.4$ and $+\SI{9.6}{\percent}$, median \SI{0.27}{\percent}. \textbf{A
sub-grid fit locates the crest to about \SI{10}{\percent} at best}, which is why
this chapter reports the crest as a range

Reporting a single density would also overstate what this result is about. The
crest belongs to the $n=3$ and $n=4$ pair specifically. An
$H_\alpha/H_\gamma$ diagnostic, which uses $n=3$ against $n=5$, has its worst
density at $\SI{7.2e12}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, a factor $2.68$ lower
(\texttt{findings\_10}~\S7.3). The correct form of words is that the
$H_\alpha/H_\beta$ ratio is least reliable near a certain density, never that
the Balmer diagnostic is.

There is no interior maximum in temperature anywhere in the defended range. The
error falls monotonically from \SI{18.07}{\percent} at
\SI{2.02}{\electronvolt} to \SI{4.55}{\percent} at \SI{8.29}{\electronvolt}
along the crest column, both per $+\SI{4.81}{\percent}$ step
(\texttt{verify\_ridge\_mechanism.py}). The apparent peak near
\SI{2}{\electronvolt} is the floor of the scope restriction of
Section~\ref{sec:scope}, not a feature of the plasma.

\section{Why a percentage is not a result}
\label{sec:step_convention}
% QUESTION: is the headline error a property of the plasma, or of the step size
% chosen to probe it?
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Every number in the previous section is attached to a $+\SI{4.81}{\percent}$
temperature step, and $\SI{4.81}{\percent}$ is not a physical quantity. It is
what one gets from 50 logarithmically spaced grid points between $1$ and
\SI{10}{\electronvolt}. An examiner is entitled to ask what happens for an ELM
that changes the temperature by \SI{10}{\percent}, and a chapter whose headline
is a percentage has no answer.

The answer, over the grid steps available, is that the error grows fast:

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{$\epsplat$ against the size of the imposed heating step, in grid
intervals: the grid maximum with its location, and the same-point series at
three fixed operating points. Values from
\texttt{validation/reservoir\_gain/reservoir\_gain.csv}
(\texttt{verify\_reservoir\_gain.py}, heating direction). The one- and
two-interval maxima sit at $[0,4]$ and the four-interval maximum at $[0,5]$,
all on the cold edge, and none is quantitative (Section~\ref{sec:scope}).}
\label{tab:step_dependence}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
  \toprule
  step & step in $\Te$ & grid max.\ of $\epsplat$ & at $[0,4]$ & at $[15,3]$ & at $[23,5]$ \\
  \midrule
  1 & $+\SI{4.81}{\percent}$  & \SI{38.7}{\percent} at $[0,4]$  & \SI{38.7}{\percent}  & \SI{18.1}{\percent} & \SI{6.36}{\percent} \\
  2 & $+\SI{9.85}{\percent}$  & \SI{87.4}{\percent} at $[0,4]$  & \SI{87.4}{\percent}  & \SI{38.2}{\percent} & \SI{13.9}{\percent} \\
  4 & $+\SI{20.68}{\percent}$ & \SI{190.4}{\percent} at $[0,5]$ & \SI{187.3}{\percent} & \SI{81.1}{\percent} & \SI{32.9}{\percent} \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

A factor $4.3$ in fractional step size moves the error at a fixed operating
point by a factor $4.8$ at $[0,4]$ ($0.3869$ to $1.8732$), $4.5$ at $[15,3]$
and $5.2$ at the benchmark $[23,5]$; the grid maximum moves by a factor $4.9$
only because its location shifts from $[0,4]$ to $[0,5]$ at four intervals. Quoted
bare, \SI{38.7}{\percent} is a statement about a grid, and the sentence
\emph{``the quasi-steady-state error reaches \SI{39}{\percent}''} is not
falsifiable because it does not say what it is \SI{39}{\percent} of.

The way out is not a better choice of step. It is to report the response
function instead of one of its values, which is what
Section~\ref{sec:exact_decomposition} does. That the error more than doubles
between the first and second rows of Table~\ref{tab:step_dependence}, while the
quantity introduced there moves by \SI{2}{\percent}, is the reason for
preferring the second object to the first.

\subsection{The split has exactly two channels}
\label{sec:slow_subspace}

Everything in this section and the two that follow rests on a partition made in
Chapter~\ref{ch:theory}: one slow state, the ground state $1s$, and $42$ fast
ones. That partition deserves an argument rather than an assertion, because
there is an obvious candidate for a second slow state.

The $2s$ level cannot decay to the ground state by emitting one dipole photon,
because both states have zero orbital angular momentum and a single dipole
photon must carry one unit of it away. It decays instead by emitting two
photons at once, at $8.229\,\si{\per\second}$, a lifetime of
\SI{0.12}{\second} against roughly a nanosecond for the $2p$ level beside it.
If $2s$ held population on the slow timescale it would be a second reservoir:
the supply sum $\nF = \bm{a}\,\ngs + \bm{c}\,\nion$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:two_supplies} would gain a third term, the logistic of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:f_logistic} would not merely be displaced but would change shape,
and the bound of Section~\ref{sec:bound} would not follow.

In a plasma $2s$ is not depopulated by radiation. Proton impact mixes it into
$2p$, which then decays promptly, and that route carries between \SI{99.92}{\percent}
and \SI{99.99}{\percent} of the $2s$ loss budget across the grid, the floor
falling at \SI{10}{\electronvolt} and the ceiling at the cold edge
(\texttt{make\_story\_figures.py}). At the thinnest and coldest point on the grid,
$\Te = \SI{1}{\electronvolt}$, $\nel = \SI{e12}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, the
total $2s$ loss rate is
$|L_{2s,2s}| = 1.2564\times10^{9}\,\si{\per\second}$ against a slow
eigenvalue $|\lambda_0| = 0.0149\,\si{\per\second}$, a separation of
$8\times10^{10}$.

That argument leans on a proton-impact rate coefficient, so it is worth having
one that does not. Delete the $\ell$-mixing entirely and leave $2s$ with
nothing but two-photon emission: $8.229\,\si{\per\second}$ is still $553$
times faster than $|\lambda_0|$ at the same corner. \textbf{On this grid $2s$
cannot be slow under any assumption about $\ell$-mixing whatever.} The density at which
proton $\ell$-mixing out of $2s$ falls to the two-photon rate, so that the
collisional route stops dominating, solves
$\nel\sum_i K^{\ell\text{-mix}}_{i,2s} = \SI{8.229}{\per\second}$ and lies
between $6.6\times10^{3}$ and \SI{1.06e4}{\per\cubic\centi\metre} over the
temperature grid, median \SI{7.9e3}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}. That is $8.10$
decades below the bottom of the operating range.

The direct spectral test agrees and supplies the number to quote. Over all
$400$ grid points the smallest ratio of $|\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda|$ over the
excited block $\Lmat_{FF}$ to $|\lambda_0|$ is $86.5$, at
$\Te = \SI{10}{\electronvolt}$, $\nel = \SI{e15}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$,
and the slow eigenvector is the ground state's at every point.

That last statement needs its norm, because the two norms of
Section~\ref{sec:two_clocks} disagree about it more sharply than
anywhere else in this thesis. In the \emph{raw} eigenvector the largest
component is the ground state at all $400$ grid points, with the $2s$ component
at most $2.05\times10^{-3}$ of it and typically $10^{-5}$: the population the
slow mode moves sits in $1s$ and nowhere else. In the
\emph{population-scaled} eigenvector $2s$ reaches $0.996$ of the ground state's
entry at the coldest, thinnest corner. \textbf{That second figure is not
evidence of a second reservoir; it is the opposite.} It says the fractional
response of $2s$ tracks the ground state's to within
\SI{0.4}{\percent}, which is what being slaved to a single reservoir means. An
independent reservoir would move on its own timescale and show up as a
\emph{difference}, not as agreement. There is no near-degenerate second slow
mode. That point and that value are the
same stress point and essentially the same quantity as the timescale-separation
minimum $\Msep = 86.8$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:M_range}, computed from the excited
block rather than from the full matrix, so the two are a consistency check on
each other and not two results. \textbf{The worst-case separation is $86.5$,
and that is the figure that describes the grid}; the benchmark value
$\Msep = 9982$ describes one point and overstates the margin by two orders of
magnitude.

All four measurements in this subsection are produced by
\texttt{src/validation/verify\_2s\_not\_slow.py} and stamped in
\texttt{validation/slow\_subspace/}, which carries one row per grid point and
raises if the largest raw component of the slow eigenvector is ever anything
but the ground state. The two-photon rate is the one number there not read from
the pipeline: the radiative array holds $A(2s\to1s) = 0$ exactly, because
two-photon decay is not an E1 transition and was never entered, so the matrix
this thesis solves is more pessimistic about $2s$ than the atom is.

\subsection{A better variable than the departure coefficient}

Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} worked in $\bdep = \ngs/(\Zsb{1}\nel\nion)$, because
that is the variable in which the observable's response has the closed form of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:R_of_b}. Here it is better to use the reservoir itself,
\begin{equation}
  u \;\equiv\; \frac{\ngs}{\nion} \;=\; \bdep\,\Zsb{1}(\Te)\,\nel ,
  \label{eq:u_def}
\end{equation}

\noindent a dimensionless ground-state population per unit ion density. At
fixed $(\Te,\nel)$ the two are proportional, so $\mathrm{d}\ln u =
\mathrm{d}\ln\bdep$ and the sensitivity of
Section~\ref{sec:ground_fed_fraction} carries over unchanged. The reason for
the change of variable is that a temperature step moves $\Zsb{1}$: comparing a
pre-step $\bdep$ with a post-step one would mix a change in the reservoir with a
change in the Saha--Boltzmann normalisation, and $u$ has no normalisation to
move. That confusion is not hypothetical. An earlier version of the analysis
reported in this section made exactly it, and the resulting contamination in
$\Delta\ln\bdep$ was comparable to the quantity being measured.

\textbf{Assumption.} Equation~\eqref{eq:u_def} treats $\nion$ as a fixed
reservoir, unchanged by the step, as throughout this thesis
(Section~\ref{sec:rate_equation}). This removes the freedom for the ionisation
balance to respond, and it is what makes $u$ numerically equal to the
ground-state population in the solved system. Chapter~\ref{ch:limits} returns to
what a mobile ion population would change, and bounds it: the total bound
population per unit $\nion$ at the benchmark is $8.925\times10^{-4}$, so
freezing $\nion$ costs at most \SI{0.09}{\percent}
(\texttt{findings\_10}~\S B.4).

\subsection{The decomposition}

Equation~\eqref{eq:R_of_b} makes the post-step observable a ratio of two affine
functions of $u$, so the logarithmic sensitivity of
Section~\ref{sec:ground_fed_fraction} is $\mathrm{d}\ln\Robs/\mathrm{d}\ln u =
\ffrac{3}-\ffrac{4}$, the difference of the two ground-fed fractions.
Write $u^-$ for the reservoir the plasma arrives with, the
\emph{pre-step} equilibrium value $u^{\mathrm{CRE}}(\Te^-)$ that the excited
manifold is frozen onto during the plateau, and $u^+$ for the \emph{post-step}
value $u^{\mathrm{CRE}}(\Te^+)$ that the lookup table assumes. Then $\Rpe$ is the
post-step operator evaluated at $u^-$, and $\Rcre$ is the same operator at
$u^+$. Integrating between the two gives, with no approximation,
\begin{equation}
  \ln\frac{\Rpe}{\Rcre}
  \;=\; \int_{\ln u^+}^{\ln u^-}\!\!\big(\ffrac{3}-\ffrac{4}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\ln u
  \;=\; \overline{S}\;\Delta\ln u ,
  \label{eq:exact_decomposition}
\end{equation}

\noindent where
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\ln u \;\equiv\; \ln\frac{u^+}{u^-}
  \label{eq:dlnu_def}
\end{equation}

\noindent is the reservoir displacement, written post-step over pre-step so that
$\Delta$ carries its usual sense of new minus old, and
\begin{equation}
  \overline{S} \;\equiv\;
  \frac{1}{\Delta\ln u}\int_{\ln u^+}^{\ln u^-}\!\!
  \big(\ffrac{3}-\ffrac{4}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\ln u
  \label{eq:Sbar_def}
\end{equation}

\noindent is the mean sensitivity over the interval the reservoir traverses.
Both factors are dimensionless.

\paragraph{The sign convention, stated once and used throughout.} The
ground-fed fraction of $n=3$ exceeds that of $n=4$ at every point of this grid,
so the integrand of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Sbar_def} is positive everywhere and only the
direction of travel changes sign. Heating depletes the ground state, so
$u^+ < u^-$, the interval runs forward, and $\Delta\ln u < 0$ while the integral
is positive. Cooling refills it, so $\Delta\ln u > 0$ while the interval runs
backwards and the integral is negative. \textbf{In both directions
$\overline{S} < 0$, and in both directions $G < 0$}, the latter because
$\Delta\ln u$ and $\Delta\ln\Te$ always carry opposite signs. Measured over all
$2288$ rows of \texttt{reservoir\_gain.csv} this holds without exception:
$\overline{S}$ runs $-0.4963$ to $-0.0355$ and $G$ runs $-14.52$ to $-2.64$.

The product $\overline{S}\,\Delta\ln u$ is therefore positive for heating and
negative for cooling, and $\epsplat$ is positive in both because
Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_plateau_exact} takes a modulus: heating gives
$\mathrm{e}^{+}-1 > 0$ and cooling gives $1-\mathrm{e}^{-} > 0$. Nothing
observable depends on the orientation, since reversing it flips $\Delta\ln u$
and $\overline{S}$ together and leaves $\epsplat$ unchanged, which is why the
tables report $|\overline{S}|$ and $|G|$. The convention is spelled out because
an earlier draft defined $\Delta\ln u$ with the opposite orientation from the
script that computes it; the two disagreed about the sign of $G$ while agreeing
about every error.

Since $\ffrac{3},\ffrac{4}\in[0,1]$ by Section~\ref{sec:ground_fed_fraction},
and $\overline{S}$ is an average of their difference, $|\overline{S}| < 1$
everywhere. That is the universal bound and it follows from positivity alone.
The sharper $|\overline{S}| \le \tanh(|\Delta|/4)$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:peak_sensitivity} is a separate statement resting on the
unit-width logistic, and Section~\ref{sec:bound} keeps the two apart
deliberately; an earlier draft here derived the sharper one from
$\ffrac{m}\in[0,1]$, which yields only the weaker.
Exponentiating gives the plateau error:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\ \epsplat = \left|\,\mathrm{e}^{\overline{S}\,\Delta\ln u} - 1\,\right|\ }
  \label{eq:eps_plateau_exact}
\end{equation}

A single scalar error has become a product of two quantities that can be
measured independently: how strongly the observable responds to its reservoir,
and how far the reservoir moved. Nothing in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact_decomposition} says which of them varies across the
operating map, and Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} determines that.

\subsection{The reservoir gain, and why it is the object to report}
\label{sec:gain}

The second factor still contains the step. Splitting it,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\ln u \;=\; G\,\Delta\ln\Te ,
  \qquad
  G \;\equiv\; \frac{\Delta\ln u}{\Delta\ln\Te}
  \;\xrightarrow[\;\Delta\ln\Te\to0\;]{}\;
  \frac{\partial \ln u^{\mathrm{CRE}}}{\partial\ln\Te}\bigg|_{\nel},
  \label{eq:gain_def}
\end{equation}

\noindent defines the \emph{reservoir gain} $G$: the factor by which a
fractional change in electron temperature is amplified into a fractional change
in the equilibrium ground-state population. It is a \emph{secant}, taken along
the CRE curve between the two endpoints of the step, and it is defined that way
deliberately, so that Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_of_step} below is exact rather than
first-order; it tends to the partial derivative on the right as the step
closes. Under the convention of Eq.~\eqref{eq:dlnu_def} the secant and its
limit carry the same sign, which they did not in an earlier draft. It is
dimensionless, it is negative throughout the grid because heating ionises the
ground state away, and unlike $\epsplat$ it is very nearly a
property of the operating point rather than of the experiment done to it. Substituting into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_plateau_exact},
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\ \epsplat = \Big|\exp\!\big(\overline{S}\,G\,\Delta\ln\Te\big)-1\Big|\ },
  \qquad
  \lim_{\Delta\ln\Te\to0}\frac{\epsplat}{\big|\Delta\ln\Te\big|}
  = \big|\overline{S}\,G\big| .
  \label{eq:eps_of_step}
\end{equation}

\noindent The small-step limit on the right is the useful form: it is the
diagnostic error per unit fractional temperature excursion, with the excursion
divided out. It is written as a ratio rather than as
$\mathrm{d}\epsplat/\mathrm{d}\ln\Te$ for a reason that is easy to miss.
$\epsplat = |\mathrm{e}^{z}-1|$ has a corner at $z=0$: its one-sided
derivatives there are $+1$ and $-1$, so the two-sided derivative does not exist
and a limit written that way is not well posed at the point it is being taken.
The ratio of moduli is, and it is the quantity actually wanted. The
\emph{signed} logarithmic response is smooth and gives the same content without
the modulus,
\begin{equation}
  \lim_{\Delta\ln\Te\to0}
  \frac{\ln\!\big(\Rpe/\Rcre\big)}{\Delta\ln\Te}
  \;=\; \overline{S}\,G ,
  \label{eq:signed_response}
\end{equation}

\noindent which is the form to differentiate if anything downstream needs a
derivative. In fact Eq.~\eqref{eq:signed_response} holds as an exact identity at
every finite step, not merely in the limit, because $\overline{S}$ and $G$ are
both defined as secants over the step actually taken; writing it as a limit is
weaker than the truth and the limit is kept only to make the comparison with a
derivative explicit.

At one grid interval $|\overline{S}G|$ is $1.31$ at the benchmark, $3.54$ at
$[15,3]$ and $6.96$ at $[0,4]$, computed from the values in
Table~\ref{tab:gain}. \textbf{Those are one-interval secants, not the zero-step
limit.} Extrapolating linearly in the number of intervals to $k\to0$ gives
$1.25$, $3.66$ and $7.41$, so the secant sits $5.1$, $3.5$ and
\SI{6.4}{\percent} from the tangent at the three points, and the two should not
be quoted interchangeably. Read the middle one as: in the worst conditions this
thesis defends, a \SI{1}{\percent} temperature transient costs the Balmer
inversion about \SI{3.5}{\percent} in the line ratio.

\subsection{Use the exact form, not the linearisation}

The first-order form $\epsplat \approx |\overline{S}\,\Delta\ln u|$ is what one
would write down first, and it is adequate for a small step and wrong for a
large one. At the four-interval step it returns \SI{59.4}{\percent} where
Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_plateau_exact} gives \SI{81.1}{\percent} at $[15,3]$
(\texttt{verify\_ridge\_mechanism.py}). The exponential costs nothing and this
chapter uses it throughout.

It would be convenient if the linearisation erred in one direction only, so
that it could be quoted as a bound. It does not, and an earlier draft of this
work claimed that it did. Which way it errs depends on which linearisation is
meant. For the \emph{endpoint} form actually computed at
\texttt{verify\_plateau\_gridmap.py}, line 212, the ratio of measured to
predicted error over the heating map runs $0.8417$ to $1.3891$ with median
$1.0310$: the linearised expression understates in 219 of 392 heating steps and
overstates in the rest, and at the benchmark it overstates, with ratio $0.9459$
(\texttt{findings\_10}~\S B.3). \textbf{Any statement about the linearisation
must name which linearisation it is about.}

A final caution about what the decomposition can and cannot be used for.
Equation~\eqref{eq:exact_decomposition} is an identity, so comparing its two
sides tests nothing: a check that $\epsplat$ equals
$|\mathrm{e}^{\overline{S}\Delta\ln u}-1|$ can fail only through arithmetic
error. What the identity permits is an \emph{attribution}. The two factors are
separately measurable, so one may ask which of them carries the structure the
maps show. That is a weaker kind of statement than a derivation, and it is the
kind made in Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{A bound that no atomic dataset can exceed}
\label{sec:bound}
% QUESTION: is there a limit on the diagnostic error that holds independently of
% the rate coefficients, and what exactly does it constrain?
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Section~\ref{sec:ground_fed_fraction} showed that in the logarithmic reservoir
variable both ground-fed fractions are the same unit-width logistic curve,
displaced by $\Delta = \ln[(a_3/a_4)/(c_3/c_4)]$, and that their difference
therefore has the closed-form maximum
$\max|\ffrac{3}-\ffrac{4}| = \tanh(|\Delta|/4)$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:peak_sensitivity}. Since $\overline{S}$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:Sbar_def} is an average of $\ffrac{3}-\ffrac{4}$ over an
interval, it inherits the bound:
\begin{equation}
  \big|\overline{S}\big| \;\le\; \tanh\!\left(\frac{|\Delta|}{4}\right) \;<\; 1 ,
  \qquad \Delta \text{ evaluated on the \emph{post-step} operator.}
  \label{eq:Sbar_bound}
\end{equation}

\noindent The qualifier is load-bearing and was missing from an earlier draft.
$\overline{S}$ averages $\ffrac{3}-\ffrac{4}$ along the post-step curve, so the
displacement that caps it is the post-step one. Tested over all $2288$ rows of
\texttt{reservoir\_gain.csv}, the bound holds with \textbf{zero} exceptions when
$\Delta$ is read on the post-step operator, and is violated at $101$ of them,
\SI{4.4}{\percent}, worst excess $0.0087$, when it is read on the pre-step
operator that labels each row. The natural reading of a row label is the wrong
one here, which is why the condition belongs in the equation rather than in the
reader's head.

Three properties make this worth stating separately from the algebra that
produced it.

\textbf{There are two bounds here, and only one of them is independent of the
atomic data.} The distinction is easy to lose and an examiner will not lose it.

The \emph{universal} bound is $|\ffrac{3}-\ffrac{4}| < 1$, which follows from
$\ffrac{3},\ffrac{4} \in [0,1]$ and therefore from nothing but the positivity of
$\bm{a}$ and $\bm{c}$ established in Section~\ref{sec:ground_fed_fraction}. It
holds for any non-negative rate data whatever, for any pair of shells, and it is
what licenses the amplification statement below. Nothing about hydrogen enters
it.

The \emph{sharp} bound $\tanh(|\Delta|/4)$ is stronger and is not universal in
the same sense. Its \emph{functional form} is structural: the $\tanh$ is a
consequence of the logistic having unit width, and the width is unity because
the excited populations are exactly affine in $\ngs$, which is a property of the
linear system and not of any rate coefficient
(\texttt{findings\_10} ADDENDUM~B.1). But its \emph{numerical value} is set by
$\Delta = \ln[(a_3/a_4)/(c_3/c_4)]$, and those four coefficients are the atomic
data. \textbf{Change the rate set and the cap moves.} Every property of the
42-state network enters exactly there, and only there.
What \emph{is} established about $\Delta$ is invariance under two specific
choices, which is a narrower claim than robustness to the rate data and should
not be read as it. $\Delta$ is invariant under rescaling the $\ngs/\nion$
normalisation, verified exactly under factors of $10^{10}$ and $10^{-7}$, and is
insensitive to the $\ell$-weighting used to bundle a shell: population sum,
$p$-states, $d$, $s$ and statistically weighted give $1.94561$, $1.94474$,
$1.94447$, $1.94471$ and $1.94574$ at the benchmark, a spread of
\SI{0.07}{\percent} (\texttt{findings\_10}~\S B.5). The individual switching
points $x_3$ and $x_4$ and the fractions $\ffrac{p}$ are \emph{not} invariant
under that rescaling; only their separation is.

\textbf{Its physical content is a statement about amplification.} Because
$|\mathrm{d}\ln\Robs/\mathrm{d}\ln\bdep| < 1$ everywhere, integrating over any
excursion gives $|\Delta\ln\Robs| \le |\Delta\ln\bdep|$. A line ratio can never
respond to the ground-state reservoir faster, in relative terms, than the
reservoir itself moves. A stale reservoir cannot be amplified in logarithmic
space, only attenuated.

\textbf{The bound is loose in general and tight at the crest.} At the benchmark
$|\overline{S}| = 0.2288$ against a cap of $0.4503$, so the sensitivity is at
\SI{51}{\percent} of what the atomic structure permits. At $[23,3]$, one column
into the crest, $|\overline{S}| = 0.4261$ against a cap of $0.4394$, that is
\SI{97}{\percent} (both from the table in \texttt{findings\_10}~\S B.2;
the percentages are arithmetic on those two columns). Where the error is
largest, the diagnostic is running at essentially the maximum sensitivity to the
reservoir that \emph{this rate set} permits.

An earlier draft added that no refinement of the rate coefficients could make
that worse. \textbf{That does not follow and is withdrawn.} Sitting at
\SI{97}{\percent} of the cap says there is little headroom \emph{beneath} the
cap; it says nothing about where the cap itself would move, and the cap moves
with $\Delta$, which is atomic data. A refinement can raise
$\tanh(|\Delta|/4)$ and can move $u^{\mathrm{peak}}$ relative to the operating
point, in either direction.

What is measured, and it is less than the withdrawn claim, is insensitivity to
two specific perturbations of the rate set. Rebuilding $\Lmat$ with a
density-consistent Debye cutoff at every grid point moves $\ffrac{3}-\ffrac{4}$
by at most \SI{0.42}{\percent}, and scaling every $\ell$-mixing rate over a range
of $\times0.1$ to $\times10$ moves it by under \SI{0.3}{\percent}
(Section~\ref{sec:lmixing}). Both concern $\ell$-mixing.

One refinement of the excitation dataset is now tested, and it is the one the
benchmark of Section~\ref{sec:atomic_uncertainty} makes available. Substituting
the RMPS rate coefficients wherever they exist and re-solving the steady state
moves $\Delta$ by \SI{9.1}{\percent} on average over the $448$ defended pairs
and by at most \SI{17.8}{\percent}, and moves the cap with it, by
\SI{8.4}{\percent} on average
(\texttt{src/validation/verify\_reservoir\_gain\_anderson.py}; an earlier draft
quoted \SI{7.1}{\percent} and \SI{6.5}{\percent}, which are means over every
row of the file, all three step sizes and the cold edge included). So the cap
does move, by about the size the withdrawn claim ignored.

What does not move is the \emph{headroom}. $|\overline{S}|$ and its cap fall
together: the median of $|\overline{S}|$ as a fraction of its own cap goes from
\SI{80.4}{\percent} to \SI{78.2}{\percent}, and its maximum from
\SI{99.9}{\percent} to \SI{99.7}{\percent}. The bound is violated at none of
the $338$ points under either rate set. Running at \SI{97}{\percent} of the cap
is therefore not an artefact of this particular rate set, which is more than the
withdrawn claim was entitled to and less than it asserted.

The recombination channel can be substituted too, and it behaves differently
enough to be worth separating. $\alpha_{\mathrm{3BR}}$ is not independent
data: Eq.~\eqref{eq:alpha_3BR} builds it from the ionisation coefficient by
detailed balance, so substituting the ionisation dataset substitutes the
three-body recombination with it. The model takes ionisation from CCC TICS
below $n=9$ and from Lotz above it, so Lotz is an independent dataset already
in hand, and running the whole table from Lotz instead replaces both at once.
Over the states where CCC supplies the model, Lotz is larger by a factor
between $1.16$ and $5.32$, median $2.13$ --- a far bigger perturbation than the
excitation substitution, and one that changes the ionisation loss on the
diagonal of $\Lmat$ as well as the source vector.

It moves $\Delta$ by \SI{1.9}{\percent} and the cap with it, and $\epsplat$
by \SI{16.4}{\percent}, over the same $448$ pairs. The disparity is the point,
and it is sharpest in what each swap does to the headroom. Over the $338$
heating pairs on which the bound was checked, the excitation swap moves
$|\overline{S}|$ by \SI{8.4}{\percent} and the cap by \SI{6.7}{\percent} on
average, in absolute terms, and moves them together: the ratio
$|\overline{S}|/\tanh(|\Delta|/4)$ changes by \SI{4.3}{\percent} on average
and by \SI{-1.4}{\percent} at the median pair. The ionisation swap moves
$|\overline{S}|$ by \SI{17.8}{\percent} on average inside a cap that moves by
\SI{1.5}{\percent}, and it does so in both directions: the ratio changes by
\SI{17.7}{\percent} on average, downward at $62$ percent of the pairs and by
\SI{-8.3}{\percent} at the median pair, while the median of the headroom
distribution itself is unchanged, \SI{80.4}{\percent} before and
\SI{80.5}{\percent} after. The ionisation data do not push the operating
point one way; they scramble where each pair sits beneath its cap. The two
datasets act on different objects. $\Delta$ is a ratio of ratios,
$(a_3/a_4)/(c_3/c_4)$, and a change in the ionisation coefficients that is
similar at $n=3$ and $n=4$ largely cancels in it; what such a change moves is
where the reservoir sits, which is the variable
Section~\ref{sec:ridge_conditional} identifies as the one that controls
$|\overline{S}|$. \textbf{Refining the excitation data moves the cap;
refining the ionisation data moves the operating point beneath it, pair by
pair and in both directions.}
Under all four rate sets the bound is violated at none of the $338$ points.

Radiative recombination is the one input with no second dataset here. It is
Johnson's Eq.~(7), quoted as accurate to \SI{5}{\percent} against exact
Karzas--Latter Gaunt factors, and scaling it by that much --- a sensitivity
test, not a substitution, and not to be quoted as a dataset comparison ---
moves $\epsplat$ by \SI{1.1}{\percent} and at most \SI{2.4}{\percent}.
Radiative recombination is not where this uncertainty lives.

\subsection{What the bound does not constrain}
\label{sec:inversion_jacobian}

A diagnostician does not suffer an error in $\Robs$. They measure $\Robs$ and
invert it. At known $\nel$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:inversion_jacobian} makes the inferred
temperature error the ratio of two derivatives, both taken at fixed $\nel$:
the sensitivity of the observable to the reservoir over its sensitivity to
temperature. Equation~\eqref{eq:Sbar_bound} constrains the numerator. It says
nothing about the denominator, and Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} deferred the
denominator to this chapter. If $\nel$ is not independently known the relevant
object is the $2\times2$ Jacobian of Eq.~\eqref{eq:inversion_2x2} instead, which
this thesis does not measure.

The denominator vanishes. Scanning $\Te$ at
$\nel = \SI{5.18e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, $\epsstep$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_step_def} passes through a minimum of $4.9\times10^{-5}$ near
$\Te = \SI{6.87}{\electronvolt}$, with values $7.18\times10^{-4}$,
$3.79\times10^{-4}$, $4.9\times10^{-5}$, $2.74\times10^{-4}$ and
$5.89\times10^{-4}$ at $6.251$, $6.551$, $6.866$, $7.197$ and
\SI{7.543}{\electronvolt} (\texttt{verify\_plateau\_gridmap.py}). Since
$\epsstep$ is the fractional distance between the two tabulated answers either
side of the step, a zero means $\Rcre$ is stationary in temperature there:
$\partial\ln\Robs/\partial\ln\Te = 0$.

\textbf{The $n=3/n=4$ ratio is locally non-invertible for temperature near
\SI{6.9}{\electronvolt} at this density.} There, a bounded error in the
observable produces an unbounded error in the inferred temperature, and the
plateau error is not the relevant failure mode at all: the diagnostic has no
temperature resolution to lose. This is a separate failure from the one the
rest of the chapter maps, it is arguably the more severe of the two for a
practitioner, and it is visible only because $\epsstep$ was computed and kept
rather than discarded as uninteresting.

The scan above is one density column, and the locus is not confined to it.
Evaluating $\Rcre$ on the full grid and locating where
$\partial\ln\Robs/\partial\ln\Te$ changes sign
(\texttt{verify\_crest\_subgrid.py},
\texttt{validation/crest\_subgrid/eps\_step\_locus.csv}) puts a stationary point
inside the operating range in \textbf{six of the eight density columns}, at
\SIrange{5.18}{8.69}{\electronvolt}, moving to higher temperature as the density
rises. It leaves the range only at the two highest densities,
$\nel \ge \SI{3.73e14}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, where $\Robs$ is monotone in
$\Te$ throughout and the smallest $\epsstep$ on the column is
$1.1\times10^{-3}$ rather than the $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-4}$ found elsewhere.

Where the locus exists the diagnostic is locally blind: a temperature excursion
of any size moves the tabulated ratio by almost nothing, so the inversion cannot
see it at all. \textbf{That is a different failure from the one this thesis
maps, and a worse one for a practitioner, because it has no transient in it and
persists in steady state.} Its consequence for the inversion is measured in
Section~\ref{sec:inversion_measured}: $44$ of the $218$ heating pairs in the
defended scope reach their inferred temperature only by crossing it. It also bears on
the non-uniqueness of the inversion: a stationary point in $\Robs(\Te)$ is a
fold, and on either side of it two temperatures return the same ratio.

\subsection{The inversion, measured}
\label{sec:inversion_measured}

Equation~\eqref{eq:inversion_jacobian} is a linearisation, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} promised the exact finite-step form wherever a number
is quoted. The exact form is a root-find. At known $\nel$, the temperature the
table returns for the plateau observable $\Rpe$ is every $\Te^{*}$ on that
density column at which $\Rcre(\Te^{*},\nel) = \Rpe$, and the
inferred-temperature error is $\ln\Te^{*} - \ln\Te^{+}$, with $\Te^{+}$ the
true post-step temperature. That root-find was run at every one of the $784$
point-and-direction pairs of Section~\ref{sec:eps_definitions}, with the same
step rule, the same plateau-window rule and the same three linear solves
(\texttt{verify\_inversion\_error.py}). It reproduces the stamped
\texttt{plateau\_gridmap.csv} with a worst difference of zero, and on the two
monotone density columns the sign of every inferred error equals the sign of
the observable error times the sign of $\mathrm{d}\ln\Rcre/\mathrm{d}\ln\Te$,
$180$ of $180$, a check an inverted interpolation would fail at every pair.

\textbf{In more than a third of the defended scope the table has no solution
inside its own domain.} Of the $448$ pairs at $\Te\ge\SI{2}{\electronvolt}$,
$166$ carry a plateau ratio below the smallest value the table holds anywhere
on that density column, so no $\Te^{*}$ exists within the tabulated $1$ to
\SI{10}{\electronvolt}, and every one of them is a cooling step. That count
belongs to the table's edges as much as to the plasma, and it is quoted with
the domain attached wherever it appears. A further $44$, all heating, place
$\Te^{*}$ on the far side of a stationary point of
Section~\ref{sec:inversion_jacobian} from $\Te^{+}$, so the inversion reached
its answer only by crossing a fold. That leaves $238$ pairs with a unique,
fold-free answer, and those carry the numbers below.

\textbf{Over those $238$ pairs a one-interval temperature step of
\SI{4.81}{\percent} is read back as a temperature error with median
\SI{47.7}{\percent}} ($|\Delta\ln\Te^{*}| = 0.390$), a 90th percentile of
\SI{96}{\percent} and a worst case of \SI{144}{\percent}. As an amplification
of the true excursion the median is $8.3$, the 90th percentile $14.3$ and the
largest $19.0$. Including the $44$ fold-crossed pairs at their nearest root
raises the median to \SI{56}{\percent} and the amplification to $9.5$; the
fold-free figures are the ones quoted, because a nearest root on the far side
of a fold is a choice and not a measurement. Heating and cooling differ, at
medians of \SI{55}{\percent} over $174$ heating pairs and \SI{38}{\percent}
over $64$ cooling pairs.

\textbf{What is a theorem.} At fixed $\nel$ the table is the composition
$\Rcre(\Te) = R\big(\Te, u^{\mathrm{CRE}}(\Te)\big)$, so its slope splits by
the chain rule into the manifold's own response and the response through the
reservoir,
\begin{equation}
  \Sigma \;\equiv\; \frac{\mathrm{d}\ln\Rcre}{\mathrm{d}\ln\Te}
  \;=\; P + SG,
  \qquad
  P \equiv \Big(\frac{\partial\ln R}{\partial\ln\Te}\Big)_{\!u},
  \quad
  S \equiv \Big(\frac{\partial\ln R}{\partial\ln u}\Big)_{\!\Te}
    = \ffrac{3}-\ffrac{4},
  \quad
  G \equiv \frac{\mathrm{d}\ln u^{\mathrm{CRE}}}{\mathrm{d}\ln\Te},
  \label{eq:slope_two_channels}
\end{equation}
exactly and locally. Here $S$ carries the natural sign of a derivative and is
positive for the $(3,4)$ pair; the interval average $\overline{S}$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:Sbar_def} is the same quantity with the opposite sign, by its
limits of integration, and the two must not be confused. On the plateau the
reservoir is frozen, so to first order the observable moves by
$\delta\ln\Rpe \simeq P\,\delta\ln\Te$, while the table, which knows only
equilibrium reservoirs, can account for any movement only at its own slope
$\Sigma$. Inverting the plateau ratio against the table therefore returns
$\delta\ln\Te^{*} \simeq (P/\Sigma)\,\delta\ln\Te$, and the error in the
inferred temperature is
\begin{equation}
  \delta\ln\Te^{*} - \delta\ln\Te \;\simeq\; -\frac{SG}{\Sigma}\,\delta\ln\Te
  \;=\; -\frac{SG}{P+SG}\,\delta\ln\Te ,
  \label{eq:inversion_local}
\end{equation}
which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:inversion_jacobian} with the reservoir displacement
written as $\delta\ln u = G\,\delta\ln\Te$. The amplification of the true
excursion is $|SG/\Sigma|$, and it diverges where $\Sigma = P + SG \to 0$.
The stationary point of Section~\ref{sec:inversion_jacobian} is therefore not
a second, unrelated failure: it is the limit in which the two channels cancel
exactly.

\textbf{The finite step carries the same decomposition, exactly.} Over one
grid interval $D = \Delta\ln\Te$ the three quantities are secants, and all
three come out of the three solves that give $\epsplat$:
\begin{equation}
  \overline{P} = \frac{\ln\big[\Rpe/\Rcre(\Te^{-})\big]}{D},
  \qquad
  \overline{SG} = \frac{\ln\big[\Rcre(\Te^{+})/\Rpe\big]}{D},
  \qquad
  \overline{\Sigma} = \frac{\ln\big[\Rcre(\Te^{+})/\Rcre(\Te^{-})\big]}{D}
  = \overline{P} + \overline{SG},
  \label{eq:slope_secants}
\end{equation}
where the identity is a telescoping sum of logarithms. The reservoir secant is not a product of two separately averaged
factors: by Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact_decomposition} and Eq.~\eqref{eq:gain_def} it
equals $-\overline{S}\,G$ exactly, the sign being the one just noted, and
\texttt{verify\_operator\_slope\_decomposition.py} confirms both identities to
$5\times10^{-15}$ at all $784$ pairs.

\textbf{What is measured.} Two signs hold at every one of the $448$ pairs in
scope: $\overline{P} > 0$, the $(3,4)$ ratio rising with the temperature of the
manifold at a frozen reservoir, and $\overline{SG} < 0$, which is $S > 0$
times $G < 0$, heating emptying the reservoir. Neither is derived here; both
are properties of this operator over the defended range. The net slope is
negative, $\overline{\Sigma} < 0$, at $359$ of the $448$, and the local slope
at the post-step node at $360$. The two channels are nearly equal and
opposite: medians $|\overline{P}| = 1.07$ and $|\overline{SG}| = 1.12$, while the
per-pair cancellation ratio $|\overline{\Sigma}|/|\overline{P}|$ has median
\SI{6.8}{\percent} across the $448$ pairs. The median of $|\overline{\Sigma}|$
is separately $0.091$; dividing the printed medians gives \SI{8.5}{\percent},
a coarser summary of the same cancellation, and the two should not be
conflated.

\textbf{Direction: a local result, and its two boundary exceptions.} With
$P > 0$ and $SG < 0$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:inversion_local} places the inferred
temperature on the side the plasma came from wherever $\Sigma < 0$: colder
than $\Te^{+}$ on heating, hotter on cooling. The exact root-find agrees with
no exception, $172$ of $172$ heating pairs and $64$ of $64$ cooling pairs with
$\Sigma(\Te^{+}) < 0$. Where $\Sigma(\Te^{+}) > 0$, above the fold, the same
equation predicts a hotter branch on heating. There are $46$ such heating
pairs in scope. At $44$ the tabulated ratio does not climb by the size of the
offset before the table ends at \SI{10}{\electronvolt}, and the nearest root
is a colder one reached across the fold, which is the fold-crossed class
above; at the other $2$ a colder root lies inside the step interval itself. A
local slope predicts the local direction and not the existence of a root over
a finite interval, so that last statement is empirical and belongs to this
table and this step.

\textbf{Amplification: the ratio of the reservoir term to the residual
slope.} A one-interval transient displaces the ratio by $|\overline{SG}|$
while the table reads the displacement back at $|\overline{\Sigma}|$, so the
finite-step prediction for the amplification is
$|\overline{SG}|/|\overline{\Sigma}|$, large wherever the two channels nearly
cancel. Against the exact root-find over the $238$ fold-free pairs it has a
rank correlation of $0.93$ and overstates, the median of the pair-by-pair
ratio being $1.26$; the local form $|\overline{SG}|/|\Sigma(\Te^{+})|$
overstates by \SI{18}{\percent}. Per column the exact median amplification
runs $8.2$, $10.2$, $12.7$, $12.7$, $10.7$ and $6.8$ across the six columns
from $10^{12}$ to \SI{1.39e14}{\per\cubic\centi\metre} that carry a stationary
point, then $3.4$ and $1.7$ on the two monotone columns above; over the $56$
pairs at $\nel\ge\SI{3.73e14}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ the median
inferred-temperature error is \SI{16}{\percent} and the largest
\SI{19}{\percent}. The columns that carry the fold are the columns that carry
the amplification because both are the same cancellation, one at its limit.
The off-table cooling cases are the same physics again: at those $166$ pairs
$|\overline{P}|$ exceeds $|\overline{\Sigma}|$ by a median factor $19.7$,
against $4.8$ where a cooling root exists, and a ratio falling that much
faster than the equilibrium curve leaves the curve's range.

\textbf{Why $P > 0$: a decomposition, not a proof.} At fixed $u$ the shell
populations are affine, so with $A_m = \partial\ln a_m/\partial\ln\Te$ and
$C_m = \partial\ln c_m/\partial\ln\Te$,
\begin{equation}
  P \;=\; \underbrace{\ffrac{3}A_3 - \ffrac{4}A_4}_{P_a}
        \;+\; \underbrace{(1-\ffrac{3})\,C_3 - (1-\ffrac{4})\,C_4}_{P_c},
  \label{eq:P_channels}
\end{equation}
exactly. Over the step, with $A_m$ and $C_m$ as secants, the ground-fed
coefficients rise with temperature and the recombination-fed coefficients fall
at every pair: $A_m > 0$ and $C_m < 0$ at $448$ of $448$. Given those signs,
$P_a > 0$ if and only if $(\ffrac{3}/\ffrac{4})\,/\,(A_4/A_3) > 1$, and it
is, at $448$ of $448$, median $2.9$ and never below $1.2$: the ground-fed
channel is the larger share of $n=3$ by more than $A_4$ exceeds $A_3$. And
$P_c > 0$ if and only if
$[(1-\ffrac{4})/(1-\ffrac{3})]\,/\,(|C_3|/|C_4|) > 1$, which holds at $426$
of $448$, median $2.0$, and fails at $22$, all at
$\nel = \SI{e15}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ between $2.0$ and
\SI{3.4}{\electronvolt}, where both shells are almost entirely
recombination-fed ($\ffrac{3} = 0.08$, $\ffrac{4} = 0.01$), the share factor
is close to unity, and the sign is decided by $|C_3|$ exceeding $|C_4|$. The
sum $P_a + P_c$ is positive at all $448$ and reproduces $\overline{P}$ to a
median \SI{3.7}{\percent}, a 90th percentile of \SI{13}{\percent} and a worst
case of \SI{35}{\percent}; the residual is the second-order part of the exact
finite-step form, the logarithm of a mixture, which the script verifies to
$10^{-14}$. So $P > 0$ is an empirical property of the present operator over
the defended range, associated with the unequal supply fractions of the two
shells, and not a general consequence of $\ffrac{3} > \ffrac{4}$ alone;
twenty-two pairs already refuse the stronger channel-by-channel claim. Every
number in this paragraph is stamped in
\texttt{validation/operator\_slope\_decomposition/}.

Three cautions travel with these numbers. They are single-parameter: $\nel$ is
taken as known, and the two-parameter Jacobian of Eq.~\eqref{eq:inversion_2x2}
is still not measured. They are plateau values and not the time averages of
Section~\ref{sec:persistence}: they say what the table returns while the
reservoir is displaced, not what an exposure of a given length records. And
they inherit the frozen-reservoir conditional of Chapter~\ref{ch:limits}
exactly as $\epsplat$ does, because they are $\epsplat$ pushed through the
table. One definitional choice was tested and does not matter: interpolating
the table linearly in $(\Te, R)$ instead of $(\ln\Te, \ln R)$ moves the
fold-free median by \SI{0.01}{\percent}. Every figure in this subsection is
stamped in \texttt{validation/inversion\_error/} against the same
\texttt{L\_grid} and \texttt{S\_grid} hashes as the rest of the chapter.

\section{Where the error is largest, and why}
\label{sec:mechanism}
% QUESTION: which physical quantity puts the maximum of the error map where it
% is, and is it the same quantity on both axes?
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The maps of Section~\ref{sec:error_maps} show where the diagnostic error is
large. They do not say why, and a map without a mechanism is a lookup table
that cannot be extrapolated past the grid that produced it.

The answer has two halves and they disagree. The structure along the density
axis and the structure along the temperature axis have different causes, and
reading them as one effect, which the appearance of a single crested surface
invites, gets both wrong.

\subsection{Across density: the operating point, not the span}
\label{sec:density_mechanism}

Evaluating both factors of Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact_decomposition} at every grid
point settles which of them locates the crest
(\texttt{verify\_ridge\_mechanism.py}, over $\Te = 2$--\SI{9}{\electronvolt}
and all eight density columns). The column maximising $\epsplat$ is the column
maximising $|\overline{S}|$ in $32$ of $32$ temperature rows for a one-interval
step and $31$ of $31$ for a four-interval step; the column maximising
$|\Delta\ln u|$ coincides with the error maximum in $0$ of $32$ and $0$ of $31$.

Coincidence counts are weak evidence on their own, because a quantity that
barely varies still has its maximum somewhere. The amplitudes settle it. Across
the three decades of density $|\overline{S}|$ varies by a factor $7.11$ and
$|\Delta\ln u|$ by a factor $1.01$. \textbf{A quantity that changes by one per
cent cannot produce a crest in which the error changes by a factor of seven},
wherever its own maximum falls.

That settles \emph{which factor}. It does not settle why that factor has an
interior maximum, and here the obvious explanation is wrong.
Equation~\eqref{eq:Sbar_bound} caps the sensitivity at $\tanh(|\Delta|/4)$, so
the natural guess is that $\Delta$ itself peaks near
$\SI{2e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$: that the two supply channels are most
widely separated there, and the sensitivity follows. That guess was written
down in advance by an independent re-derivation of Chapter~\ref{ch:theory},
together with a structural argument for why $\Delta$ should collapse at high
density, namely that in local thermodynamic equilibrium (LTE) both channels
would give the same Boltzmann shape and $\Delta$ would go to zero.

because the model is open, with $\Svec_F$ an externally imposed source not tied
to $\nion$ by the Saha relation, the two channels cannot merge by construction
(\texttt{findings\_10} ADDENDUM~B.2).

\textbf{The maximum sits where $\ln(u^{\mathrm{CRE}}/u^{\mathrm{peak}})$ changes
sign.} The physical reading is direct. Both $\ffrac{3}$ and $\ffrac{4}$ rise
from zero to unity as the reservoir grows, and they switch from
recombination-fed to ground-fed at different reservoir strengths, so their
difference vanishes in both supply limits and peaks between them. The
sensitivity curve is fixed. Raising the density slides the plasma's own CRE
reservoir along it, from the ground-fed side at low density, through the
mixed-supply region, to the recombination-fed side at high density. The crest
is where that slide crosses the peak.

Two consequences follow, and both matter. First, this is a statement about
where the operating point sits, not about how sensitive the diagnostic could
be, and it replaces the weaker description of the density axis as
sensitivity-dominated: both are true, but only the first says why. Second, it
forbids the inference that the effect disappears at high density. The cap does
not close; the plasma merely walks off the peak. On a grid extending further in
density it would walk back on.

\subsection{Three independent tests of the mechanism}
\label{sec:griem_tests}

The account above predicts more than the shape of one curve, and each
prediction can fail.

\textbf{The crest must move with the shell pair, and by a predicted amount.}
The reservoir strength at which a shell switches from recombination-fed to
ground-fed is a version of Griem's LTE
criterion~\cite{Griem1963,Griem1997}, which places the boundary between
collisionally coupled and radiatively decoupled levels at a density scaling as
$n^{-17/2}$ in the principal quantum number. Moving the diagnostic from the
$(3,4)$ pair to the $(4,5)$ pair should therefore move the crest by
$(5/4)^{8.5} = 6.7$. Measured: $7.2$. The argmax of $|\ffrac{n}-\ffrac{n+1}|$
at $\Te = \SI{2.02}{\electronvolt}$ runs $\SI{3.73e14}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$
for $(2,3)$, $\SI{1.93e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ for $(3,4)$ and
$\SI{2.68e12}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ for $(4,5)$, with $(5,6)$ and above
falling off the bottom of the grid (\texttt{verify\_ridge\_mechanism.py}).

\textbf{The absolute density must match, not just the ratio.} Griem's LTE
criterion evaluated at $n=4$ and $\Te = \SI{2}{\electronvolt}$ gives
$\SI{2.05e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ against a measured crest at
$\SI{1.93e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, a \SI{6}{\percent} difference.

\textbf{An independent diagnostic must put the boundary level in the same
place.} \texttt{verify\_boundary\_descent.py} computes, without reference to
$\ffrac{3}$ or $\ffrac{4}$, the effective principal quantum number at which the
collisional and radiative rates balance. At the crest density it returns
$\bar n = 3.08$ to $3.25$ at every temperature on the grid
(\texttt{validation/boundary\_descent.csv}), that is, between $n=3$ and $n=4$,
which is exactly the pair whose supply fractions differ most.

\textbf{What would have refuted the mechanism, and did not occur:} the crest
failing to move with the shell pair, or moving the wrong way. A competing
explanation was refuted outright. The suggestion that both shells become
ground-fed at high density because collisional coupling dominates predicts
$\ffrac{3},\ffrac{4}\to1$; measured at $\Te = \benchTe$,
$\nel = \SI{e15}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, they are $0.071$ and $0.011$, so
both shells become \emph{continuum}-fed instead
(\texttt{findings\_10}~\S2).

\subsection{Alternatives that would produce the same appearance}
\label{sec:ridge_alternatives}

An interior maximum on a coarse grid can be manufactured in several ways, and
the attribution above does not by itself exclude them.

\emph{Grid resolution.} The crest occupies one density column and the grid is
spaced by a factor $2.683$, so its location is known only to that factor. What
is established is that the maximum is interior, not at either end of the
density range, and that it does not move between temperature rows above
\SI{1.15}{\electronvolt}. Refining the density grid requires rebuilding the
rate matrix and revalidating every number in Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} against
the new one; future work could determine the location more precisely.

\emph{Sub-grid drift.} A parabolic fit in $\ln\nel$ puts the peak at
$\SI{3.66e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ at \SI{1}{\electronvolt} and
$\SI{1.51e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ at \SI{9.5}{\electronvolt}, a range of
$2.56\times$, which drifts \emph{downward} with temperature while Griem's
criterion predicts an upward drift as $\sqrt{\Te}$, a factor $3.2$
(\texttt{findings\_10}~\S7.2). The whole drift is smaller than one grid
interval and therefore unresolved, so the crest should be described as constant
to within a factor $2.68$ over $1$ to \SI{10}{\electronvolt}, never as fixed.
The likely reason for the sign is that the neutral fraction $\ngs/\nion$
collapses across the grid, dragging the supply crossover down in density faster
than the Griem boundary drags it up, which is consistent with
Section~\ref{sec:density_mechanism} but has not been separated from it.

\emph{Step-size convention.} A crest whose position depended on the size of the
imposed step would be an artifact of that choice. It does not: the error
maximum falls on the same density column for a one-interval and a four-interval
step in $31$ of the $31$ temperature rows both can reach, and so does the
maximum of $|\overline{S}|$ (\texttt{verify\_ridge\_mechanism.py}). This tests
steps of fixed \emph{fractional} size; a step of fixed absolute size in
electron-volts spans a different number of grid intervals at each temperature
and is a different experiment, not tested here.

\emph{Truncation.} The model resolves $n\le8$ and bundles $n=9$--$15$. The
mechanism turns on the difference between the $n=3$ and $n=4$ supply fractions,
both fully resolved, but those fractions depend on the recombination flux
cascading from above, which the bundling approximates. Two things have since
been separated here that this alternative ran together.
\texttt{verify\_bundling\_psm20.py} has now been run
(Section~\ref{sec:bundling_check}) and it answers a different question: whether
the bundled shells would be statistical in $\ell$ if the mixing were present.
They would, by three orders of magnitude. The sensitivity of the crest \emph{position} to
$n_{\max}$ is untested, and cannot be tested with the atomic data this model
has: it is carried as the second of the five open questions in
Section~\ref{sec:scope_open}.

\emph{The population set.} Two of the scripts used here apply different filters:
\texttt{verify\_plateau\_gridmap.py} excludes the $54$ points with no plateau
window, and \texttt{verify\_ridge\_mechanism.py} does not. All $54$ lie at
$j\ge4$, so the ridge script's statistics for the two highest density columns
include points at which the plateau state being computed does not physically
exist (\texttt{findings\_10}~\S B.3). The attribution of
Section~\ref{sec:density_mechanism} does not depend on those columns, but a
reader recomputing the profile from the raw output should apply the filter.

\subsection{Across temperature: a displacement effect}
\label{sec:temperature_mechanism}

The temperature dependence has the opposite explanation, and this is where
treating the two axes alike would go wrong.

Along the crest column the plateau error falls monotonically from
\SI{18.07}{\percent} at $\Te = \SI{2.02}{\electronvolt}$ to \SI{4.55}{\percent}
at \SI{8.29}{\electronvolt}, a factor $3.72$, both per $+\SI{4.81}{\percent}$
step. Decomposed through Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact_decomposition}, $|\Delta\ln u|$
contributes a factor $2.72$ of that fall and $|\overline{S}|$ only $1.29$; in
logarithmic terms the displacement carries \SI{76}{\percent} of the decay and
the sensitivity \SI{19}{\percent}. At the four-interval step the split is
\SI{72}{\percent} and \SI{13}{\percent}
(\texttt{verify\_ridge\_mechanism.py}).

The observable therefore does not become much less sensitive as the plasma
heats. \textbf{The ground state simply moves less.} In the language of
Section~\ref{sec:exact_decomposition}, the reservoir gain $|G|$ falls with
temperature, from $14.4$ at \SI{1.00}{\electronvolt} to $5.7$ at
\SI{2.95}{\electronvolt} (Table~\ref{tab:gain}), and a step of fixed fractional
size therefore displaces the reservoir progressively less.

Why the gain behaves this way is not established here. $|\Delta\ln u|$ is flat
to one per cent across three decades of density while varying by a factor $2.7$
across temperature, which implies that
$\partial\ln\ngs/\partial\ln\Te$ at collisional--radiative equilibrium is
controlled by temperature alone. \emph{[MECHANISM NOT ESTABLISHED: this is an
observation from \texttt{verify\_ridge\_mechanism.py}, not a derived result. A
natural candidate is the Arrhenius factor in the electron-impact ionisation
rate out of the ground state, whose logarithmic temperature derivative is
$\chi_H/\Te = 4.6$ at the benchmark against a measured $|G| = 5.7$, but the
comparison has not been carried through and the two need not be equal.]}

\subsection{Three explanations this work does not support}
\label{sec:withdrawn}

Three readings of the crest are natural and do not survive the numbers. Each is
recorded because it is among the first a reader is likely to propose, and one
of them was asserted in an earlier draft of this thesis.

\textbf{It is not a detachment criterion.} The crest sits at
$\SI{1.93e19}{\per\cubic\metre}$: a factor $5.2$ below the density band that
published edge modelling associates with a detached ITER
divertor~\cite{Guillemaut2011}, and a factor $1.6$ below the modelled upstream
separatrix density~\cite{Stangeby2023}. It falls between the two, in a region
neither source characterises. Two further facts make the identification
untenable rather than merely unsupported. At the citable detached band,
$\nel = 10^{14}$ to $\SI{e15}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ with
$\Te\ge\SI{2}{\electronvolt}$, the plateau error is at most \SI{10.4}{\percent}
per $+\SI{4.81}{\percent}$ step and the \SI{100}{\micro\second} time-averaged
estimate of Section~\ref{sec:persistence} never reaches \SI{10}{\percent}, peaking at
\SI{7.2}{\percent}. And the crest location is proportional to the assumed
neutral density, which in this model is set by a local ionisation balance with
no transport and no recycling, whereas in a divertor it is set by recycling;
Chapter~\ref{ch:limits} measures that proportionality and finds one decade in
$\ngs$ moves the crest by roughly one decade in $\nel$. The crest is
reproducible. Its identification with a named divertor operating regime is not
supported by anything in this work.

\textbf{It is not a peak in temperature.} The error decreases monotonically with
temperature throughout the defended range, as
Section~\ref{sec:temperature_mechanism} shows. The apparent ridge line at
\SI{2}{\electronvolt} in Figure~\ref{fig:eps_map} is the edge of the scope
restriction.

\textbf{It is not simply where $\bdep^{\mathrm{CRE}}$ meets the sensitivity
maximum.} That reading accounts for the density structure but fails on the
temperature axis: at the four-interval step the ratio
$\bdep^{\mathrm{CRE}}/\bdep^{\mathrm{peak}}$ passes through unity at
$\Te = \SI{4.7}{\electronvolt}$, where the error is \SI{33}{\percent} and
falling monotonically (\texttt{verify\_plateau\_gridmap.py}). The endpoint of
the reservoir trajectory is the wrong object: Eq.~\eqref{eq:Sbar_def} shows
that what enters is the mean sensitivity over the whole interval traversed.

\section{Does the transient visit the plateau?}
\label{sec:bridge}
% QUESTION: the partial-equilibrium state was constructed by freezing the ground
% state by hand. Does the time-dependent system actually pass through it?
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Everything so far is algebraic. $\Rpe$ was built by holding $\bdep$ at its
pre-step value and solving the post-step excited block, which is a construction,
not an observation. If the real trajectory never lingers near that state, the
map measures a fiction.

It does linger. Propagating the full 43-state system through the step with
\texttt{scipy.linalg.expm} and tracking $\epsCRE(t)$
(\texttt{verify\_plateau\_bridge.py}) gives, at the benchmark point and at
$[15,3]$:

\begin{itemize}
  \item The excited manifold reaches its instantaneous QSS state after
    $1.94\,\taurel$ at the benchmark and $3.72\,\taurel$ at $[15,3]$.
  \item Once settled, the state deviation from QSS is $1.3\times10^{-6}$ and
    $8.1\times10^{-8}$ respectively. These are the settled values, not the
    maxima over the plateau interval, which are $471$ and $1721$ times larger
    because the interval begins in the tail of the fast transient. An external
    review of this work quoted the maxima; the settled column is the one that
    supports the claim that the manifold has equilibrated.
  \item Over the plateau, $\epsCRE(t)$ departs from the analytic $\epsplat$,
    and the size of the departure $\max|\epsCRE(t)/\epsplat - 1|$ depends on
    the interval it is measured on. On the bridge script's measured plateau,
    which at $[15,3]$ is the shorter interval, it is \SI{3.33}{\percent} at the
    benchmark and \SI{1.28}{\percent} at $[15,3]$. On the fixed $k=30$ window
    $[30\,\taurel,\,\tauslow/30]$ used by the grid-wide census below it is
    $0.0323$ at the benchmark and $0.0301$ at $[15,3]$
    (\texttt{flat\_w30\_shell} in \texttt{validation/trajectory\_census/}).
    That flatness is what licenses quoting a single $\epsplat$ instead of a
    time-dependent error, and it is measured rather than assumed.
\end{itemize}

The propagator itself was checked by running \texttt{expm} against
\texttt{expm\_multiply}, two independent algorithms, which agree to
$1.13\times10^{-10}$ and $3.69\times10^{-12}$. Two other checks that were run
are \emph{not} evidence and are recorded here so that nobody re-runs them: the
ODE residual is identically zero by construction, since
$\mathrm{d}\nvec/\mathrm{d}t$ and $\Lmat^+\nvec + \Svec^+$ are the same
expression evaluated twice, and the semigroup check is near-tautological for a
scaling-and-squaring implementation of \texttt{expm}, which is why it returned
exactly zero at both points.

\textbf{Scope.} The bridge test above is at two grid points. Running the same
propagation at every analysed pair (\texttt{verify\_trajectory\_census.py},
output in \texttt{validation/trajectory\_census/}) extends the dynamic chain
$\text{full transient}\to\Rqss(u(t))\to\Rpe$ to the grid. At all $680$ pairs
with a plateau window, the maximum departure of the excited manifold from the
instantaneous QSS manifold over the $k=30$ window is at most
$4.4\times10^{-5}$ (median $1.1\times10^{-6}$) against the $2\times10^{-3}$
tolerance, and sustained tracking begins within $4.84\,\taurel$ everywhere
(median $2.93$). The departure of $\epsCRE(t)$ from the analytic plateau over
the window has median $0.0022$ and maximum $0.0154$, at cool $[1,3]$. The
maximum tracking error over the window scales roughly as $0.06/\Msep$: the
median of $\Msep\,\max_W\epsQSS$ over the $680$ pairs is $0.060$, with a
range of $0.001$ to $0.427$, and the log-log correlation of $\max_W\epsQSS$
with $1/\Msep$ is $+0.945$ (\texttt{validation/trajectory\_census/},
section~A), which is consistent with the singular-perturbation expectation that the slow
eigenvector departs from the QSS manifold by $O(1/\Msep)$. The
eigen-propagation used for the census was checked against
\texttt{scipy.linalg.expm} at every pair, on the state and on the observable.
The density structure of Section~\ref{sec:density_mechanism} is a separate,
grid-wide algebraic calculation.

One honest record of a failed preregistration belongs here. The first version
of the plateau criterion required
$\epsQSS < 2\times10^{-3}$ throughout $20\,\taurel$ to $0.02\,\tauslow$, and it
\emph{failed} at $[15,3]$, reaching $2.778\times10^{-3}$ at the very end of the
window. The criterion was retained unchanged and the window was not shortened.
It failed because the ground state had begun its expected motion while the
tracking error stayed at $6.8\times10^{-7}$: the system had not left the QSS
manifold, it had moved \emph{along} it. The replacement interval definition,
used above, was not preregistered and is not described as such.

\section{How long the error lasts, and what an ELM averages}
\label{sec:persistence}
% QUESTION: does the plateau error survive averaging over a transient event of
% finite duration, or does it decay away inside the integration time?
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

A spectrometer integrates. If the error decayed on nanoseconds it would be
invisible in a \SI{100}{\micro\second} exposure, so the decay law matters as
much as the amplitude.

The error decays as a single exponential with time constant $\tauslow$. Fitting
$\epsCRE(t)$ over the decay gives $\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}/\tauslow = 1.0102$ at the
benchmark and $1.0656$ at $[15,3]$, with half-decay at $0.7002$ and
$0.7386\,\tauslow$ against $\ln 2 = 0.6931$ for a pure exponential
(\texttt{verify\_plateau\_bridge.py}). In physical units the half-life is
\SI{12.95}{\micro\second} at the benchmark and \SI{1.10}{\milli\second} at
$[15,3]$. Unlike the plateau duration, which is set by whatever tolerance
defines the end of the plateau, the half-life carries no threshold dependence.

That gives a time-average over an event of duration $\taudrive$:
\begin{equation}
  \big\langle\epsCRE\big\rangle_{\taudrive}
  \;\simeq\; \epsestim \;\equiv\; \epsplat\,\frac{\tauslow}{\taudrive}
          \left(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\taudrive/\tauslow}\right) ,
  \label{eq:elm_bound}
\end{equation}

\noindent which tends to zero as $\taudrive\to\infty$. Its other limit is
wrong: as $\taudrive\to0$ the estimate tends to $\epsplat$, whereas the true
time average tends to the step error $\epsstep$ of the rise, $0.0283$ against
$0.3869$ at $[0,4]$ and $0.0087$ against $0.0636$ at the benchmark
(\texttt{validation/trajectory\_census/}), so the estimate is valid only for
$\taudrive\gg\taurel$. One transient precedes the plateau
at the benchmark and does not enter this average: because $n=4$ relaxes faster
than $n=3$, their relative deviations from partial equilibrium cross at
$0.72\,\taurel$ and the ratio $n_3/n_4$ overshoots $\Rpe$ by
\SI{0.40}{\percent} at $1.16\,\taurel$ (\SI{2.6}{\nano\second}), carrying
$\epsCRE$ to $0.0678$ against $\epsplat = 0.0636$ before settling by
$5\,\taurel$; over $t\le10\,\taurel$ the $L^{2}$ norm of the excited-block
deviation from the frozen-reservoir state never exceeds its initial value at
any of the $784$ pairs (maximum growth factor $0.9998$), so the overshoot
belongs to the ratio and not to the state. Over longer times that norm does
rise again, by up to a factor $1.536$, as the reservoir drift carries the
state toward the new equilibrium; that is the slow decay, not a transient.
The overshoot is absent at $[15,3]$, and the transient before $50\,\taurel$
moves the \SI{100}{\micro\second} average at the benchmark by
$-4.0\times10^{-5}$ relative (\texttt{validation/trajectory\_census/},
section~A).

\textbf{It is written with $\simeq$ rather than $\ge$, and that is a
correction.} Equation~\eqref{eq:elm_bound} was constructed as a conservative
lower bound, on the reasoning that the observable cannot decay faster than the
slowest mode, and earlier drafts of this thesis called it one throughout. Direct
43-state eigen-propagation at every pair (\texttt{verify\_trajectory\_census.py},
\texttt{validation/trajectory\_census/}) shows it is close to the true average
and not a bound on it. Over the $448$ pairs above \SI{2}{\electronvolt} the
ratio of the true \SI{100}{\micro\second} average to $\epsestim$ runs from
$0.9791$ to $1.0222$ (median $0.9998$, below unity at $226$ of the $448$), and
at \SI{506}{\micro\second} from $0.9671$ to $1.0377$. The ratio below unity at
the worst point $[0,4]$, $0.999978$, is not a quadrature artefact: raising the
sampling from $400$ to $1600$ per decade changes the five averages of
Table~\ref{tab:lowerbound} by at most $4.7\times10^{-6}$ relative and the
$[0,4]$ average by $2.7\times10^{-10}$ (\texttt{validation/trajectory\_census/},
section~B). Its deficit of $2.2\times10^{-5}$ is the net of two contributions
of opposite sign: the first $50\,\taurel$, during which the error is $\epsstep$
rather than $\epsplat$, lower the average by $5.2\times10^{-5}$ relative, and
the slow-mode mechanism below raises it by the remainder. An inequality with a known
counterexample is not an inequality, and $\epsestim$ is what its name says: a
single-slow-mode estimate, within \SI{2.2}{\percent} of the true average at
\SI{100}{\micro\second} and \SI{3.8}{\percent} at \SI{506}{\micro\second} over
the grid.

The spectral gap of Section~\ref{sec:qss_is_exact} is why a single exponential
comes this close: the slow mode projects strongly onto the observable at every
pair and no second mode contributes. The residual departure is not a transient.
$\epsCRE = |R(t)/\Rcre - 1|$ is a ratio whose denominator, the $n=4$
population, relaxes on the same $\tauslow$ as the numerator. With $\delta$ the
slow-mode amplitude in the H$\beta$ channel relative to its CR-equilibrium
value, the ratio of true average to estimate tends to
$(1+\delta)\ln(1+\delta)/\delta = 1+\delta/2-\delta^{2}/6+O(\delta^{3})$
for $\taudrive\gg\tauslow$.
Heating gives $\delta>0$ and the estimate reads low; cooling gives $\delta<0$
and it reads high. Over the warm pairs $\delta$ runs from $-0.22$ to $+0.30$,
which is why the extreme ratios are a cooling minimum and a heating maximum in
the same density column at neighbouring temperatures, cool $[49,1]$ and heat
$[48,1]$ at \SI{100}{\micro\second}.

\textbf{What the demotion costs the census is two pairs, and it is worth
computing rather than waving at.} Counting the same $448$ pairs on the true
\SI{100}{\micro\second} average instead of on $\epsestim$ moves the census from
$45$ to $43$, worst $0.1753$ at heat $[15,3]$. No pair enters. The two that
leave, cool $[22,3]$ at $0.09949$ and cool $[23,2]$ at $0.09999$, sit at and
just below the threshold; the second misses it by $1.5\times10^{-4}$ relative,
so the propagated count is $43$, with $44$ within the numerics. At
\SI{506}{\micro\second} the propagated count is $24$ on the shell ratio and
$23$ on the $A$-weighted line ratio. \textbf{Every census in this chapter
should therefore be read as $45$ on the estimate and $43$ propagated.}

  \midrule

  \SI{250}{\micro\second} & 33 & $0.1664$ \\

\textbf{The count nearly halves and the worst case barely moves.} Over a
factor $7.5$ in event duration the count falls from $45$ to $20$ while the worst
case falls from \SI{17.5}{\percent} to \SI{14.4}{\percent}, a relative change of
\SI{18}{\percent}. At the ITER-extrapolated duration the defensible count is
\textbf{$24$ of $448$, worst \SI{15.3}{\percent}}, and that is the figure an
ITER-relevant statement should use. The maps and every derived quantity in this
chapter are computed at \SI{100}{\micro\second} and are labelled with it; the
$506$-microsecond census is a strict subset of the $100$-microsecond one,
because the estimate falls monotonically with the window at every point, so no
conclusion drawn about the $45$ fails to apply to the $24$.

Two further sensitivities belong with the census. The count depends strongly on
the threshold: at $5$, $10$ and \SI{20}{\percent} it is $348$, $202$ and $61$
over the same $680$ pairs, so the count should always be quoted with the
threshold that produced it. The worst case does not reference the threshold at
all and is unaffected by it. And the $104$ points excluded for having no plateau window
contain no breakdowns whatever, all sitting at $\Te\ge\SI{3.24}{\electronvolt}$
with a maximum estimate of $0.0011$, so the exclusion does not hide anything;
including them changes $202/680$ to $202/784$.

\paragraph{A detector records a ratio of integrated brightnesses, not the mean
of the instantaneous error.} A spectrometer with exposure $\taudrive$
integrates each line separately and reports
$\int_0^{\taudrive} j_\alpha\,\mathrm{d}t \big/ \int_0^{\taudrive} j_\beta\,\mathrm{d}t$,
a brightness-weighted mean of the instantaneous ratio, which is not the time
average of $\epsCRE$ that the census counts. That observable was computed
at every pair with the augmented-matrix exponential, which gives
$\int_0^{\taudrive}\nvec\,\mathrm{d}t$ without quadrature and to the round-off
of the matrix exponential; at the benchmark and \SI{100}{\micro\second} it
agrees with trapezoidal quadrature of $\mathbf{w}\cdot\nvec(t)$ on the sampled
trajectory to $2.7\times10^{-7}$ (\texttt{verify\_trajectory\_census.py}).
Across the $448$ pairs the error of the exposure ratio against the
CR-equilibrium ratio agrees with the time average of $\epsCRE$ to within
\SI{1.2}{\percent} at \SI{100}{\micro\second} (ratio of the two errors $0.9901$
to $1.0114$) and to within \SI{2.2}{\percent} at \SI{506}{\micro\second}
($0.9806$ to $1.0217$); that \SI{2.2}{\percent} is the agreement between two
definitions of the averaged error, and is distinct from the
\SI{2.2}{\percent} by which $\epsestim$ departs from the true average at
\SI{100}{\micro\second}. The exposure ratio gives the same census: $43$ of $448$ at \SI{100}{\micro\second} on both the
shell ratio and the $A$-weighted line ratio, and $24$ and $23$ at
\SI{506}{\micro\second}. The agreement is empirical rather than structural. At
$98$ of the $784$ pairs the signed error changes sign during the rise, so
cancellation inside the exposure integral is possible in principle; all $98$
have $\epsplat\le0.064$ and none is a census member.

\paragraph{The plateau window itself is a free parameter, and it moves only one
of the two things.} The window is defined by a separation factor $k$, taken as
$30$ throughout, so that a point is analysed only where
$k\,\taurel < \tauslow/k$. That choice is imposed rather than measured, and its
effect was swept at $k = 10$, $30$ and $100$ before this chapter was written
(\texttt{verify\_plateau\_window.py} and the two re-runs stamped in
\texttt{validation/plateau\_slowmode\_w10/} and
\texttt{\ldots\_w100/}). The result separates cleanly.

\emph{Magnitudes do not move at all.} At the benchmark $\epsplat$ is
$0.32902966$ at $k=10$ and $0.32902966$ at $k=30$, identical to eight figures,
and $0.24075077$ at the cold corner under both. That is not a numerical
coincidence: $\epsplat$ is a frozen-reservoir linear solve evaluated at one
instant, so it cannot depend on the width of a window it is not averaged over.
Sampling the trajectory across the window and extrapolating back to $t=0$
returns the analytic value to \SI{0.005}{\percent}.

\emph{Membership does move.} $k$ decides which points have a resolvable plateau
at all, and it bites: at $k=100$ the benchmark itself is excluded, needing
$\Msep > 10^4$ and having $4856$. \textbf{So $k$ sets the denominator of every
census and never the numerator's magnitude}, and a census quoted without its $k$
is as incomplete as one quoted without its threshold. Every count in this
chapter uses $k=30$.

\subsection{Two structural objections, tested}

The estimate of Eq.~\eqref{eq:elm_bound} uses $\tauslow$, and $\tauslow$ in
this model is the relaxation time of an \emph{open} system: the ground state
loses atoms to an ion reservoir that is held fixed and never gives them back.
An artificially long $\tauslow$ would inflate every entry in
Table~\ref{tab:elm_census}. Rebuilding the operator with an exactly
particle-conserving 44th state and recomputing the whole census changes the
count from $202$ to $200$ and the worst case from
\SI{38.68}{\percent} to \SI{38.56}{\percent}; above \SI{2}{\electronvolt} it
changes nothing at all, $45$ of $448$ (\texttt{findings\_10}~\S3.1). The
closure factor on $\tauslow$ reaches $41$ only where $\tauslow$ already exceeds
the event duration by three orders of magnitude, and is $1.00$ wherever
$\tauslow$ is comparable to $\taudrive$, which is where it could matter.
Chapter~\ref{ch:limits} states the caveat that this closure adds an ion
reservoir without closing the electron balance and without adding transport.

The second objection is that a step is an idealisation and a real ELM has a
finite rise time. Replacing the step by a linear ramp of duration
$t_{\mathrm{ramp}}$, integrating the full 43-state system through
$\Lmat(t) = (1-f)\,\Lmat^{-} + f\,\Lmat^{+}$ with
$f = \min(t/t_{\mathrm{ramp}},1)$, and reading the error at the start of the
plateau window gives, at $[15,3]$, the fractions $0.9995$, $0.9954$, $0.9555$
and $0.6469$ of the step's plateau error for
$t_{\mathrm{ramp}}/\tauslow = 10^{-3}$, $10^{-2}$, $10^{-1}$ and $1$
(\texttt{verify\_ramp\_plateau.py}, \texttt{validation/ramp\_plateau/}). A
single-pole system driven by a ramp reaches the fraction
$\mathrm{De}\,(1-\mathrm{e}^{-1/\mathrm{De}})$ of its step response, with
$\mathrm{De} = \tauslow/t_{\mathrm{ramp}}$, which is $0.9995$, $0.9950$,
$0.9516$ and $0.6321$ at the same four ratios: within \SI{0.5}{\percent} of the
43-state result for $\mathrm{De}\ge10$, and \SI{2.3}{\percent} low at
$\mathrm{De}=1$, where the 43-state value itself moves a further
\SI{1.7}{\percent}, from $0.6469$ to $0.6576$, between linear and log-linear
interpolation of the operator along the ramp
(\texttt{validation/ramp\_plateau/}, log-linear sensitivity block; at
$t_{\mathrm{ramp}} = 10^{-2}\tauslow$ the same change is $+1.6\times10^{-4}$),
so the fraction there is $0.65\pm0.01$. During a linear ramp the ground-state
reservoir completes the fraction
$1 - \mathrm{De}\,(1-\mathrm{e}^{-1/\mathrm{De}})$ of its total excursion, the
lag of a single pole behind a ramp, which for large $\mathrm{De}$ expands to
$(t_{\mathrm{ramp}}/\tauslow)\,(\tfrac12 - \tfrac{1}{6\,\mathrm{De}}
+ \tfrac{1}{24\,\mathrm{De}^{2}} - \ldots)$. Per unit $t_{\mathrm{ramp}}/\tauslow$
the 43-state system at $[15,3]$ gives $0.4984$ at $\mathrm{De}=100$ against the
exact $0.4983$, and $0.4841$ at $\mathrm{De}=10$ against $0.4837$; at
$\mathrm{De}=1$ the reservoir completes $0.3737$ of its excursion against the
exact $0.3679$, where the two-term truncation gives $0.3333$; at
$\mathrm{De}=1000$ the drift is $5\times10^{-4}$ of the excursion and the
43-state value is \SI{1.3}{\percent} above the exact form
(\texttt{validation/ramp\_plateau/}, lag-law block). The agreement is
established at these four values of $\mathrm{De}$ only. The relevant Damk\"ohler-like ratio is $\tauslow/\taudrive$,
which is $2332$ at $[0,4]$, and not $\taurel/\taudrive \approx 10^{-5}$, which
describes the suppression of the fast excited-manifold error and has nothing to
say about the plateau; at $\mathrm{De} = 2332$ the plateau is suppressed by
\SI{0.02}{\percent}. The step idealisation is safe, and the ratio that makes it
safe is $\tauslow/t_{\mathrm{ramp}}$, not $\taurel/t_{\mathrm{ramp}}$.

\subsection{Stepping temperature and density together as a two-parameter perturbation stress test}
\label{sec:joint_step}

Every map above steps $\Te$ at fixed $\nel$, and an ELM raises both. The test
here moves the two parameters by whole grid intervals at once; it is a stress
test of the two-channel framework, not a prescription for the path an ELM
takes through the $(\Te,\nel)$ plane. A
spot check at the benchmark had suggested this runs in the model's favour, the
time-averaged estimate falling from $0.0117$ to $0.0030$ when the density steps up
with the temperature, because a higher $\nel$ shortens $\tauslow$. That spot
check is reproduced exactly by the full map, and it is not representative.

\paragraph{The framework extends without amendment, and that is the first
result.} Nothing in the derivation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_plateau_exact} refers to
\emph{what} displaced the reservoir. Running $4968$ cases over steps of $0$,
$\pm1$ and $\pm2$ temperature indices crossed with $0$ and $\pm1$ density
indices, the two-channel superposition holds to a worst relative residual of
$3.075\times10^{-14}$, the same figure the temperature-only map reports, and
$\epsplat = |\exp(\overline{S}\,\Delta\ln u)-1|$ holds to
$3.7\times10^{-16}$ (\texttt{verify\_joint\_step\_map.py}). The sensitivity is a
property of the observable and the displacement is a property of the plasma,
whichever axis moved.

\paragraph{The gain acquires a second component, and it is additive.} What does not
survive unchanged is the second factorisation, $\Delta\ln u = G\,\Delta\ln\Te$,
which has one axis in it. Measuring the two gains at each starting point as
secants over the single-axis steps of one grid interval, $+\SI{4.81}{\percent}$
in $\Te$ at fixed $\nel$ and a factor $2.68$ in $\nel$ at fixed $\Te$,
\begin{equation}
  G_T = \frac{\Delta\ln u}{\Delta\ln\Te}\bigg|_{\nel,\;\Delta\ln\Te = 0.0470},
  \qquad
  G_n = \frac{\Delta\ln u}{\Delta\ln\nel}\bigg|_{\Te,\;\Delta\ln\nel = 0.987},
  \qquad
  \Delta\ln u \simeq G_T\,\Delta\ln\Te + G_n\,\Delta\ln\nel ,
  \label{eq:gain_gradient}
\end{equation}

\noindent the additive prediction reproduces the measured $\Delta\ln u$ to a
median of \SI{0.20}{\percent} and \SI{0.54}{\percent} at the 90th percentile
over $2716$ joint steps. Only $26$ of them exceed \SI{5}{\percent}, and those
sit where the two contributions cancel: heating empties the reservoir while a
density rise refills it, so $\Delta\ln u$ passes through zero and a relative
error on a vanishing quantity is meaningless. Their median $|\Delta\ln u|$ is
$0.0057$ against $0.398$ elsewhere.

The two gains are very different in size. $|G_T|$ runs $2.64$ to $14.52$,
which is the same range Section~\ref{sec:gain} reports, while $|G_n|$ runs
$0.044$ to $0.164$. \textbf{The reservoir is roughly fifty times less sensitive
to density than to temperature in logarithmic terms.} It matters at all only
because the density grid is coarse: one density index is
$+\SI{168}{\percent}$ against $+\SI{4.81}{\percent}$ for one temperature index,
a factor $35$ in logarithmic step, so the two contributions end up comparable in
this particular sweep. That coarseness is a limitation of the tabulation, not a
statement about ELMs, and it is why the census below is quoted per step rather
than as one number.

\paragraph{A density change alone never breaks the diagnostic.} Of the $320$
window-passing cases at $\Delta\nel = -1$ index and the $296$ at $+1$, with the
temperature held fixed, \emph{none} exceeds the \SI{10}{\percent} threshold. The
median $\epsplat$ is \SI{2.7}{\percent} and \SI{2.6}{\percent} respectively. The
error this thesis is about is driven by temperature.

\paragraph{The census, and why the benchmark spot check misled.} Counting as
Table~\ref{tab:elm_census} does, over both temperature directions at one index
and $\Te\ge\SI{2}{\electronvolt}$:

The count barely moves, at $10.3$, $10.0$ and \SI{11.3}{\percent} of the pairs
available. \textbf{The worst case moves the other way from the spot check: it
rises by \SI{26}{\percent}, from $0.1748$ to $0.2206$, when the density steps
up.} The benchmark and the worst point respond differently because they sit on
opposite sides of the density crest of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}. Stepping up
from $\nel = \SI{1.39e14}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ moves away from the crest and
collapses $\tauslow$ from $18.5$ to \SI{5.23}{\micro\second}, so the
time-average wins; stepping up from $\SI{7.2e12}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ moves
\emph{toward} it, and $\epsplat$ rises faster than $\tauslow$ falls.

\textbf{One spot check at one point was not enough to settle the sign of this,
and it gave the wrong sign for the quantity that is reported.} The defensible
statement is that the census is insensitive to a density step of this size while
the worst case is not, and that the worst case is understated by the
fixed-density map rather than overstated.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Timescale separation does not predict the reservoir-closure error}
\label{sec:M_does_not_predict}
% QUESTION: does the standard validity criterion for the quasi-steady-state
% approximation, the timescale separation M, indicate where the reservoir-closure error is largest?
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

A modeller deciding whether to trust a QSS-based inversion reaches for
$\Msep = \tauslow/\taurel$, the stiffness ratio that licenses eliminating the
fast variables in the first place. Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} measured it from
$86.8$ to $1.73\times10^{9}$ over all $784$ pairs of the grid; over the $680$
analysed pairs on which the correlations below are computed its minimum is
$902$ (\texttt{validation/partial\_fe/}). The natural expectation is that
the diagnostic is worst where $\Msep$ is smallest.

It is not. Once the operating point is accounted for, what remains is not an
absence of information but information with the \emph{opposite} sign to the
naive expectation: the controlled partial correlation is negative in all twelve
combinations of Table~\ref{tab:partial_sweep}, median $-0.53$, and it stays
negative, $-0.617$ pooled, under saturated two-way fixed effects
(Table~\ref{tab:partial_fe}). The statement has to be read precisely. At fixed
$(\Te,\nel)$ and step direction there is exactly one $\Msep$ and one
$\epsplat$, so nothing is being compared \emph{at} a fixed point. What the
partial says is that, after the additive trends in $\log\Te$ and $\log\nel$
are removed, the residual variation of $\log\Msep$ and the residual variation
of $\log\epsplat$ are anticorrelated. Those residuals are small: two-way fixed
effects explain $R^{2} = 0.998$ of $\log\Msep$ and $0.992$ of $\log\epsplat$
in the heating direction ($0.987$ and $0.983$ pooled;
\texttt{validation/partial\_fe/}, two-way fixed-effects $R^{2}$ block), so the
partial correlates the remaining $0.2$ and $0.8$ percent of the variance.
Two-way fixed effects absorb every additive function of $\Te$ and of $\nel$,
so what remains is the $\Te$--$\nel$ interaction: the partial is a
second-order statement about that interaction, not a statement that larger
$\Msep$ means larger error. An earlier draft said $\Msep$ carried no
information at all; that is contradicted by the tables below and is
withdrawn.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering

  \label{fig:M_vs_eps}
\end{figure}

The two extrema make the point without statistics. Over the whole grid the
largest $\Msep$, $1.73\times10^{9}$, sits at the cooling step from $[1,0]$
($\Te = \SI{1.048}{\electronvolt}$ before the step,
$\nel = \SI{e12}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$) and carries
$\epsplat = \SI{12.0}{\percent}$; the coldest corner $[0,0]$ exists only in
the heating direction, with $\Msep = 9.39\times10^{8}$ and
$\epsplat = \SI{8.30}{\percent}$, the 59th percentile of the $680$ analysed
pairs. The largest error, \SI{38.7}{\percent} at $[0,4]$, sits $52\times$ away
in density at an $\Msep$ smaller by a factor $46$
(\texttt{validation/divertor\_map/divertor\_map.csv}). Those are whole-grid
numbers and both extrema lie below \SI{2}{\electronvolt}. Inside the defended
range $\Te\ge\SI{2}{\electronvolt}$ the separation persists at smaller
magnitude: the largest $\Msep$ is $2.63\times10^{6}$ at the cooling step from
$[15,0]$ with $\epsplat = \SI{7.4}{\percent}$, the largest $\epsplat$ is
\SI{18.1}{\percent} at the heating step from $[15,3]$ with
$\Msep = 2.0\times10^{5}$, the two points sit on the same $\Te$ row
$19.3\times$ apart in density, and the error at the $\Msep$ maximum is a factor
$2.5$ below the worst (\texttt{validation/m\_rank\_test/}, Part~5). Where
$\Msep$ is largest is not where the reservoir-closure error is largest.

The raw association looks strong and is a temperature proxy. Over the $680$
analysed pairs, $\mathrm{corr}(\log\Msep, \log\epsplat) = +0.76$. But
$\log\Msep$ is \SI{93}{\percent} explained by $(\log\Te,\log\nel)$ alone, and a
bare Arrhenius factor $\mathrm{e}^{\chi_H/\Te}$ built from the hydrogen
ionisation potential $\chi_H = \SI{13.6}{\electronvolt}$, which contains no
dynamics whatever, correlates with $\log\epsplat$ at $+0.71$
(\texttt{make\_ch5\_figures.py}). Both quantities rise steeply toward low
temperature for unrelated reasons, and that shared trend is nearly all of the
$+0.76$.

Removing the shared trend reverses the sign. Residualising both $\log\Msep$ and
$\log\epsplat$ on $(\log\Te,\log\nel)$ leaves a partial correlation of $+0.27$
over all pairs and $+0.33$ over the heating pairs alone; residualising on a
full quadratic basis in the same two variables leaves $-0.44$
(\texttt{make\_ch5\_figures.py}, which reports the sign of the quadratic
partial when it draws Figure~\ref{fig:M_vs_eps}; an earlier version refused
to draw the figure unless that sign was negative, which made the figure
unproducible for the one outcome that would have been informative, and that
guard was removed).

\textbf{Report the sign, not the magnitude.} The magnitude of the controlled
partial depends on the basis chosen for the control, which is a modelling
choice and not a measurement. The sign does not, and that claim is now tested
rather than asserted. Sweeping four scopes, all analysed pairs, heating only,
cooling only, and the defended range $\Te\ge\SI{2}{\electronvolt}$, against four
control bases of increasing richness, linear, full quadratic, quadratic plus an
Arrhenius term $1/\Te$, and full cubic, gives Table~\ref{tab:partial_sweep}.

The sign is a statement about residuals and must not be read as a ranking. At
fixed $\Te$, $\Msep$ falls monotonically with $\nel$ in all $98$ (row,

That argmax result is the evidence for the claim, and it is worth separating
from a weaker test that goes the other way. If the negative partial above were
a ranking, the row-wise Spearman correlation of $\Msep$ with $\epsplat$ across
the density columns would be negative in most rows. It is not. Taken over all
$50$ temperature rows of the analysed grid the median is $+0.23$, with only
$36$ percent of rows negative, and the artifact records the prediction it was written to test as
\textbf{refuted}; restricted to $\Te\ge\SI{2}{\electronvolt}$ the median falls
to $+0.07$ with $49$ percent of rows negative, which is not refutation but is
not support either. The adverse figure is the whole-grid one and it is stated
here because quoting only the defended subset would hide it. The two results
are consistent, and the reason they are is the sentence above: the row-wise
correlation is set by where the crest sits relative to the grid columns, which
is a fact about the sampling rather than about predictive skill, whereas the
partial is a statement about residuals after the operating point is removed and
the argmax comparison is a statement about location. Only the last of the three
is what a modeller reaching for $\Msep$ would actually need, and it fails at
every row.

The negative result itself is not new as a general statement.
Greenland concluded, for arbitrary collisional--radiative systems, that
validity criteria ``are not related to the equilibrium
time-scales''~\cite{Greenland2001b} and that ``the eigenvalues have secondary
importance''~\cite{Greenland2001a}. What this work adds is a quantification for one diagnostic and a
map of where the criterion misleads, which is a smaller claim than discovery and
a more useful one than assertion.

One objection to all of this is that $\Msep$ is computed from rate
coefficients known to about \SI{13}{\percent}
(Section~\ref{sec:atomic_uncertainty}), so a criterion might appear to fail only
because the quantity being tested is noisy. It does not.
Section~\ref{sec:atomic_propagation} substitutes the independent R-matrix rate
coefficients wherever they exist and re-solves the spectrum, which shifts
$\Msep$ by \SI{11}{\percent} on average. Against a quantity spanning
\num{7.30} decades that is a displacement of \SI{1.08}{\percent} of the span at
its largest, and it is very nearly a common factor: the Spearman rank
correlation between the two versions of $\Msep$ over the 400 grid operators is
$0.99993$, $\Msep$ still falls monotonically with density in all $50$
temperature rows, and the fraction of $\log\Msep$ explained by
$(\log\Te, \log\nel)$ alone is unchanged at \SI{93.7}{\percent}. Every
statement in this section is about the \emph{ordering} of $\Msep$ and about its
structure in $(\Te,\nel)$, and the substitution preserves both. The criterion
fails for a structural reason, not a numerical one.

The reason the criterion fails here is visible in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_plateau_exact}. $\Msep$ compares two \emph{rates}; $\epsplat$
measures a \emph{distance}, the product of a sensitivity and a displacement,
neither of which appears in $\Msep$. A large $\Msep$ means the excited
states equilibrate quickly with the reservoir, which
Section~\ref{sec:qss_is_exact} confirms to a part in $10^{8}$. It says nothing
about whether the reservoir is in the right place. The conclusion the data
support is the narrower one: $\Msep$ supports the fast-state QSS reduction,
and it is neither a sufficient nor a robust predictor of the reservoir-closure
error.

\section{Where the quantitative scope begins}
\label{sec:scope}
% QUESTION: this chapter restricts quantitative claims to Te >= 2 eV. Is that a
% hedge, or a boundary the model sets for itself?
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Every quantitative statement in this chapter has been restricted to
$\Te\ge\SI{2}{\electronvolt}$. Two separate things go wrong below that
boundary. Neither of them was chosen, both were measured, and they put the
boundary in the same place.

\subsection{Holding the electron density fixed stops being consistent}
\label{sec:quasineutrality}

Every operator in this chapter is evaluated at fixed $\nel$: the temperature
steps, the density does not. That is not an approximation whose cost can be made
small by care, because in a hydrogen plasma the electrons and the neutrals are
not independent quantities. Quasineutrality ties them: every ground-state atom
that ionises delivers one electron, so if the reservoir collapses the electron
density must rise by the same absolute amount.

Whether that matters depends on how much of the hydrogen is neutral. At
$\Te = \benchTe$ the plasma is almost fully ionised and the neutral population
is a small perturbation on the electron budget, so the coupling is negligible.
At $\Te = \SI{1}{\electronvolt}$ the model's own steady state is $95$ to
\SI{98}{\percent} neutral. The ground state then holds an order of magnitude
more particles than the electron population does, and a modest fractional change
in it is an enormous fractional change in $\nel$.

Imposing conservation of nuclei across a single $+\SI{4.81}{\percent}$
temperature step makes this quantitative. Writing
$x = \ngs^{\mathrm{new}}/\ngs^{\mathrm{old}}$ for the fractional collapse of
the reservoir, the electron density that quasineutrality would require is:

The first two rows are not a separate piece of physics from the error itself.
$x = 0.506$ is $|\ln x| = 0.68$, and $0.68$ is the reservoir displacement that
\texttt{plateau\_gridmap.txt} records at $[0,4]$ and that
Section~\ref{sec:exact_decomposition} identifies as the source of the
\SI{38.7}{\percent}. \textbf{The displacement that produces the cold-edge error
is exactly the displacement that makes the fixed-density assumption
inconsistent.} Over the $392$ one-step operators, $68$ of them, \SI{17}{\percent},
require $|\Delta\nel/\nel| > \SI{10}{\percent}$, and $36$, \SI{9}{\percent},
require more than \SI{100}{\percent}. The model evaluates the post-step operator
at the old $\nel$ at every one of them. Above \SI{2}{\electronvolt} the
heating-direction, fixed-$\nel$ inference never exceeds $5.4\times10^{-3}$, its
largest value there sitting at \SI{2.02}{\electronvolt}, and it first falls
below $10^{-3}$ above \SI{2.56}{\electronvolt}
(\texttt{make\_story\_figures.py}). Taken over both step directions and solved
self-consistently, the correction reaches $7.98\times10^{-3}$ at a cooling step
at $\Te = \SI{2.024}{\electronvolt}$, $\nel = \SI{1e12}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$,
and moves $\epsplat$ there by \SI{2.4}{\percent}
(\texttt{verify\_closed\_nuclei.py}). Eight parts in a thousand is still small
beside the tens of percent this chapter reports, but the stronger claim that the
correction is negligible in both directions is refuted, and it is not the one
part in a thousand an earlier draft claimed.

This closes a gap between two objections that this thesis has until now
presented as separate and separately survivable. If the plasma parcel is closed,
quasineutrality forces $\nel$ up by a factor of $10$ to $20$ at the cold end,
and every rate coefficient in the post-step operator is then evaluated at the
wrong density. If instead the parcel is fed by transport, so that $\nel$
genuinely does stay fixed while the neutrals are consumed, then the ground state
is being resupplied from outside and is not stale, which removes the mechanism
that produces the error. \textbf{The two pictures cannot both be held, and the
cold edge needs one of them.}

The cold corner also fails a cruder test: it is not a divertor state. The
model's own steady solution at $[0,4]$ gives
$\ngs = \SI{1.4669e15}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, which at a neutral temperature
equal to the electron temperature is a neutral pressure of \SI{235}{\pascal};
at $[0,7]$ it is \SI{3280}{\pascal}
(\texttt{verify\_cold\_corner\_pressure.py},
\texttt{validation/cold\_corner/}). For contrast the benchmark gives
\SI{0.058}{\pascal}.

Those want a comparison, and the comparison has to be one that was read rather
than repeated. An earlier draft set them against ``the ITER divertor design
range of roughly $1$ to \SI{20}{\pascal}'', which could not be traced to any
source and is withdrawn. Read from the ITER divertor physics basis, the
SOLPS-4.3 database for the $Q_{\mathrm{DT}} = 10$ baseline runs between a lowest
divertor neutral pressure of about \SI{2}{\pascal} and about
\SI{10}{\pascal} at strong partial detachment
\cite[Secs.~3.1.3, 4]{Pitts2019}; later high-throughput SOLPS-ITER runs extend
it to $\langle p_{\mathrm{div}}\rangle > \SI{25}{\pascal}$
\cite[Sec.~6]{Stangeby2023a}, citing \cite{Lore2022}. These are the spans of
modelled databases rather than design limits, and they are used here only as
orders of magnitude. Against the top of the baseline database, $[0,4]$ sits a
factor $24$ over and $[0,7]$ a factor $328$ over; against the extended one,
factors of $9.4$ and $131$. The benchmark sits a factor $173$ under the
baseline top.

\textbf{This is a third boundary, and it falls where the other two do.} Of the
$400$ grid points, $32$ imply a neutral pressure above \SI{25}{\pascal} and
\emph{none of them lies at $\Te\ge\SI{2}{\electronvolt}$}; the implied pressure
drops below \SI{25}{\pascal} at every density once
$\Te > \SI{1.6}{\electronvolt}$. Particle conservation, radiation transport and
now the implied gas pressure are three unrelated pieces of physics, and all
three put the floor between $1.1$ and \SI{2.4}{\electronvolt}.

These are fixed points of a zero-dimensional box with no transport and no
pumping. A real divertor's neutral density is set by recycling and pumping
rather than by local ionisation balance, which is the objection
Chapter~\ref{ch:limits} raises against the closed parcel, so the pressure is
evidence that the cold corner is outside the modelled regime rather than a
prediction of a pressure.

\subsection{The plasma is not optically thin at the cold edge}
\label{sec:optical_depth}

The rate matrix of Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} treats the plasma as optically thin:
every photon emitted escapes. For the Balmer lines that is a good assumption
over the whole grid. For the Lyman series, which terminates on the ground state
where essentially all the atoms are, it fails badly at the cold edge. Computing
the Doppler line-centre optical depth from the model's own CRE solution gives a
Lyman-$\alpha$ depth of $80.3$ per centimetre at $\Te = \SI{1.00}{\electronvolt}$,
$\nel = \SI{5.18e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, falling to $0.0255$ at
\SI{2.02}{\electronvolt} and $0.00163$ at \SI{2.95}{\electronvolt}
(\texttt{verify\_lyman\_optical\_depth.py}; Doppler width $\sqrt{2kT_n/m}$ with
$T_n = \Te$). At the cold edge the Einstein coefficients in the matrix are
wrong, as effective decay rates, by about $1.8$ orders of magnitude
self-consistently at that point, reaching about three orders at the worst point
on the grid.

Rebuilding the matrix with self-consistent Lyman escape factors for all 14
Lyman channels, over an assumed homogeneous slab of thickness $D$, and
recomputing the whole map gives Figure~\ref{fig:trapping}.

  \caption[Lyman trapping and the restricted range]{\CapFigTrapping}
  \label{fig:trapping}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Above \SI{2}{\electronvolt} nothing moves.} The ELM breakdown count
above \SI{2}{\electronvolt} stays at $45$ of $448$ for $D = 1$, $5$ and
\SI{20}{\centi\metre}, and the worst case runs $0.1748$, $0.1746$ and $0.1740$
against $0.1748$ untrapped, a change of \SI{0.5}{\percent} across a twentyfold
range in a parameter this zero-dimensional model does not contain. At the
benchmark the plateau error moves by \SI{0.7}{\percent} over the same range.
\textbf{The $\Te\ge\SI{2}{\electronvolt}$ restriction is therefore not a hedge.
It is the measured boundary beyond which the neglected process most likely to
invalidate the result stops mattering}, which is a stronger statement than a
disclaimer and was not available before the calculation was run.

Below it, the cold-edge numbers are inflated. At $[0,4]$ the plateau error falls
from \SI{38.7}{\percent} to \SI{15.7}{\percent} at $D = \SI{1}{\centi\metre}$
and \SI{11.6}{\percent} at $D = \SI{20}{\centi\metre}$, a factor $2.5$ to
$3.3$, and which value inside that range it takes is set by the assumed slab
thickness rather than by anything the model computes. The point still exceeds
the \SI{10}{\percent} threshold at every thickness, so it still breaks down;
the \emph{magnitude} is not quotable. Below \SI{1.15}{\electronvolt} the density
column carrying the maximum wanders across three columns, a factor $7$, as a
function of $D$, so the two coldest rows cannot support a claim about where the
maximum is at all.

Chapter~\ref{ch:limits} takes up the mechanism of the fall, which is not a loss
of sensitivity, and the assumptions the slab geometry imports.

\subsection{The two boundaries coincide}

Radiation trapping and quasineutrality are unrelated pieces of physics, entering
through the Einstein coefficients and through the particle balance respectively.
They fail together. Above \SI{2}{\electronvolt} the trapped and untrapped
breakdown censuses are identical and the required density correction stays
below $5.4\times10^{-3}$; below it both grow quickly, and by \SI{1}{\electronvolt} the
first has divided the headline error by up to $3.3$ while the second has
invalidated the operator it was computed from.

Figure~\ref{fig:scope} draws both boundaries on the operating plane. The
quasineutrality contour at \SI{10}{\percent} sits at $\Te = 1.41$ to
\SI{1.50}{\electronvolt} and is almost independent of density. The
optical-depth contour at $\tau_0 = 1$ sits at $1.13$ to
\SI{1.98}{\electronvolt} at a slab thickness of \SI{5}{\centi\metre}, and
across the three thicknesses in the trapping sweep it spans $1.01$ to
\SI{2.36}{\electronvolt}. Of the 24 optical-depth crossings, 2 lie above
\SI{2}{\electronvolt}, both at the largest thickness $D = \SI{20}{\centi\metre}$
and at the two highest density columns.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig6_1_scope}
  \caption{\CapFigScope}
  \label{fig:scope}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The $\Te\ge\SI{2}{\electronvolt}$ restriction is therefore not a hedge.
Two independent tests, each with a threshold that was chosen, place the
failure of this model's own assumptions in the band from $1$ to
\SI{2}{\electronvolt}, and the restriction is the conservative working boundary
adopted above that band; it is where the neglected process most likely to
overturn the result has been shown not to.} Two of the $24$ optical-depth
crossings, both at $D = \SI{20}{\centi\metre}$, lie above it. Every headline number in this
chapter sits inside it. The cold-edge values are reported because they exist and
because suppressing them would be worse, and they are reported as what they are:
the asymptotic behaviour of a model outside its own domain of validity.

\section{What this chapter establishes}
\label{sec:results_summary}
% QUESTION: after all of the above, what can now be claimed about the cost of
% assuming ionisation balance that could not be claimed before?
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The approximation this thesis set out to test is not the one that fails.
Measured along a full 43-state trajectory, the quasi-steady-state closure is
accurate to $6.73\times10^{-9}$ at the very grid point carrying the largest
diagnostic error. The excited states keep up. What does not keep up is the
ground state, and the assumption a two-parameter lookup table makes about it is
not that the fast states equilibrate but that the plasma has finished changing.

What that assumption costs has a closed form. The plateau error factorises
exactly into a sensitivity and a reservoir displacement,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_plateau_exact}, and the displacement factorises again into a
reservoir gain and the size of the transient, Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_of_step}. The
gain moves by $5$ to \SI{6}{\percent} across a fourfold change in step size
while the error itself moves by a factor $4.9$, so the gain is the object worth
tabulating and the percentage is not. In the small-step limit the diagnostic
error per unit fractional temperature excursion is $|\overline{S}G|$: about
$1.3$ at the benchmark point and about $3.5$ at the worst point in the defended
range.

Pushed through the table, that observable error becomes an inferred-temperature
error an order of magnitude larger than the transient that caused it. Inverted
at known density, the plateau ratio has no solution within the table's $1$ to
\SI{10}{\electronvolt} domain for $166$ of the $448$ defended pairs, every one
a cooling step, and over the $238$ pairs with a unique fold-free answer a
\SI{4.81}{\percent} temperature step is read back with a median error of
\SI{48}{\percent}, on the side the plasma came from at every pair. The
amplification is the ratio of the reservoir term to the residual slope of the
table, and it is large because the manifold's own response and the reservoir's
nearly cancel, to \SI{6.8}{\percent} of either; the stationary point of
Section~\ref{sec:inversion_jacobian} is that cancellation completed. At the
two highest densities the median error is \SI{16}{\percent}. The
two-parameter inversion is not measured.

Every timescale in this chapter moves by \SI{11}{\percent} when the R-matrix
excitation set replaces the CCC one, measured in
Section~\ref{sec:atomic_propagation}; that is the sensitivity to one
substitution, not an uncertainty. It is too small to move any
conclusion drawn here, all of which rest on the ordering of $\Msep$ rather than
its value. Carried through the steady state to the observable, and
combined with the substitution of the ionisation dataset that
Section~\ref{sec:bound} describes, the two move $\epsplat$ by
\SI{15.5}{\percent} on average over the $448$ defended pairs and by at most
\SI{25.4}{\percent}, always downward at the points this chapter quotes:
\SI{6.36}{\percent} to \SI{4.80}{\percent} at the benchmark,
\SI{18.1}{\percent} to \SI{16.4}{\percent} at the ridge. The ionisation
dataset is the larger of the two levers, at \SI{16.4}{\percent} against
\SI{9.8}{\percent}, and the benchmark point is the least robust of the three.
The split between the two structural coefficients is sharp. Under the
excitation substitution the reservoir gain $G$ is nearly invariant, moving
\SI{1.1}{\percent} on average, while the sensitivity $\overline{S}$ carries
almost all of the change at \SI{9.1}{\percent}: $G$ is a
property of how the reservoir responds to temperature, and $\overline{S}$ is
built from the ratios $a_3/a_4$ and $c_3/c_4$, which are the excitation data
themselves.

The sensitivity factor obeys a bound, $|\overline{S}| \le \tanh(|\Delta|/4) < 1$,
that follows from the structure of the linear system and not from any rate
coefficient. A line ratio cannot respond to its ground-state reservoir faster,
in relative terms, than the reservoir moves. At the crest the measured
sensitivity is at \SI{97}{\percent} of that bound.

Across the operating range the error has an interior maximum in density,
between $7\times10^{12}$ and $\SI{5e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, and no
interior maximum in temperature. That range is a whole-grid figure and its
upper end belongs to the three coldest rows; above \SI{2}{\electronvolt} every
row maximises on $\SI{1.93e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ and the subgrid vertex
spans a factor $1.35$ (\texttt{validation/crest\_subgrid/}). The density maximum is a position effect: the
separation between the two supply channels is nearly flat across density, and
what sweeps is the plasma's own reservoir sliding along a fixed sensitivity
curve, with the maximum where $\ln(u^{\mathrm{CRE}}/u^{\mathrm{peak}})$ changes
sign. The mechanism survived three independent tests, including a quantitative
prediction of how far the maximum moves when the shell pair changes, and it is a
property of the atomic system, needing no divertor geometry to produce it. It is
not detachment, and it is the $(3,4)$ pair's maximum rather than the Balmer
series'.

Inside the defensible scope, $\Te\ge\SI{2}{\electronvolt}$, $45$ of $448$
point-and-direction pairs commit a time-averaged error above \SI{10}{\percent}
over $\taudrive = \SI{100}{\micro\second}$, the worst being \SI{17.5}{\percent},
falling to $24$ of $448$ and \SI{15.3}{\percent} at the
\SI{506}{\micro\second} extrapolated to the ITER pedestal;
at citable divertor densities none do, the worst being \SI{7.2}{\percent}. That
census is unchanged across a twentyfold range in the one neglected process,
Lyman trapping, most likely to have invalidated it.

That scope boundary is the chapter's least expected result. It was adopted as a
precaution against radiation trapping and it turns out to be the boundary of
self-consistency: below \SI{2}{\electronvolt} the plasma is mostly neutral, and
conservation of nuclei then demands an electron-density change of $10$ to $20$
times across the same temperature step whose effect is being measured at fixed
$\nel$. The cold-edge numbers are asymptotic values of a model outside its own
domain, and this chapter reports them as such and builds nothing on them.

Finally, the criterion a modeller would reach for does not see any of this
coming. Within the defended range, timescale separation and diagnostic error
have their maxima $19.3\times$ apart in density, \SI{7.4}{\percent} against
\SI{18.1}{\percent}, and their apparent correlation reverses sign once the
operating point is controlled for. $\Msep$ compares rates; the diagnostic error
is a distance.

What the model cannot say is the subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:limits}: the plasma
is treated as optically thin where it is not, the atoms are held where they are
put, and the gas is treated as atomic where it is mostly molecular. Each of
those is quantified there rather than hedged.
