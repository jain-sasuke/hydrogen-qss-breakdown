\chapter{What the Model Says}
\label{ch:results}

Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} left the argument at a definition and a worry. The
definition: a Balmer-ratio lookup table indexed on $(\Te,\nel)$ is a table in
\emph{three} arguments with the third one hidden, and the hidden argument is
the ground-state departure coefficient $\bdep$, which takes a time $\tauslow$ to
settle after the plasma changes. The worry, which is the one the literature
carries: the excited states are what a spectrometer sees, and they may not keep
up with a plasma that changes on microseconds. Chapter~\ref{ch:validation}
established that the rate matrix these questions are asked of reproduces
independent atomic data where such data exist, and reported the two errors that
an audit of this work found and corrected.

This chapter evaluates both errors over the operating range, and the two
results are worth stating before the evidence rather than after it.

The first is a reversal. The quasi-steady-state (QSS) closure, the
approximation that the excited populations sit in instantaneous steady state
with whatever ground-state reservoir is present, is not the problem. Measured
along a full time-dependent trajectory it is accurate to eight significant
figures. What fails is the assumption that the reservoir itself has finished
moving, which is a different assumption, made at a different step of the
inversion, and never previously separated from the first.

The second is that what that failure costs is not a number. It is a function,
and the function has a closed form,
%
\begin{equation*}
  \epsplat
  = \Big|\exp\!\big(\overline{S}\;G\;\Delta\ln\Te\big) - 1\Big| ,
\end{equation*}
%
in which $\Delta\ln\Te$ is the size of the transient the reader cares about,
$G$ measures how far the ground-state reservoir is dragged by it, and
$\overline{S}$ measures how strongly the line ratio answers to the reservoir.
The last of these is bounded above by a quantity that no choice of atomic data
can push past unity. So the chapter reports the structure and the bound, and
lets a reader compute the error for their own transient. A single percentage
would be an answer to a question about the grid this thesis happens to use, and
Section~\ref{sec:step_convention} shows how badly that goes.

Both results are stated over $\Te\ge\SI{2}{\electronvolt}$.
Section~\ref{sec:scope} shows that this is not a hedge: it is the region of the
operating grid on which the model's own assumptions are mutually consistent, and
two unrelated pieces of physics place the boundary in the same place.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{What this chapter takes as given.} From
Chapter~\ref{ch:theory}: the rate equation
$\dot{\nvec} = \Lmat\nvec + \Svec\nion$ and its two timescales
$\taurel$ and $\tauslow$; the QSS elimination and its Schur complement; the two
reference states $\Rqss$ and $\Rcre$ and the two error measures
$\epsQSS$ and $\epsCRE$ built on them; the two-supply decomposition
$\nF = \bm{a}\,\ngs + \bm{c}\,\nion$; the ground-fed fraction $\ffrac{m}$, its
logistic form, and the identity
$\mathrm{d}\ln\Robs/\mathrm{d}\ln\bdep = \ffrac{3}-\ffrac{4}$. From
Chapter~\ref{ch:intro}: the operating range
$\Te = 1$--\SI{10}{\electronvolt}, $\nel =
10^{12}$--\SI{e15}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}, and the fact that an edge-localised
mode (ELM) disturbs a divertor for of order \SI{100}{\micro\second}.
The benchmark point $\Te = \benchTe$, $\nel = \benchne$, grid index $[23,5]$,
runs through this chapter as it runs through the last.

\section{What happens after a temperature step}
\label{sec:step_response}
% QUESTION: when the electron temperature changes abruptly, what does the
% 43-level population vector actually do, and on what timescales?
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Take a plasma sitting quietly at collisional--radiative equilibrium (CRE), the
steady state of the full 43-level system, and change the electron temperature
abruptly at $t=0$, holding $\nel$ fixed. Three things then happen, on three
separated timescales, and the middle one is where this thesis lives.

For the first few nanoseconds the excited manifold rearranges. Every one of the
42 excited levels is a reactive intermediate in the chemical-engineering sense:
it holds almost no population, it is created and destroyed thousands of times
faster than the species feeding it changes, and it therefore reaches a
steady state with respect to that species almost immediately. At the benchmark
point this takes $\taurel = \SI{2.28}{\nano\second}$.

For the next several microseconds nothing further appears to happen, and this
is the interesting part. The excited populations have settled, but they have
settled onto the \emph{old} ground-state population, because the ground state
holds essentially all the atoms and cannot turn over in nanoseconds. The system
occupies a state that is steady in its fast variables and stale in its slow
one. Call it \emph{partial equilibrium} (PE). At the benchmark point it lasts
until the ground state has moved, on $\tauslow = \SI{22.7}{\micro\second}$, so
the plateau is some four orders of magnitude wider than the relaxation that
built it.

Only then does the plasma reach the new collisional--radiative equilibrium that
a lookup table would have assigned to it the instant the temperature changed.
Figure~\ref{fig:trajectory} shows all three phases on a single logarithmic time
axis, at the benchmark and at the coldest grid point.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig5_6_trajectory}
  \caption{\CapFigTrajectory}
  \label{fig:trajectory}
\end{figure}

The ELM that motivates this thesis lasts of order \SI{100}{\micro\second}. It
is long against $\taurel$ by a factor of about $4\times10^{4}$ and comparable
to $\tauslow$, so a spectrometer pointed at the divertor during one spends its
integration time looking at the plateau. That is the state whose error this
chapter measures.

\subsection{Two error measures, and why the step size is part of each}
\label{sec:eps_definitions}

Section~\ref{sec:two_references} fixed the two reference states against which an
observable can be judged. Two further quantities are needed here, and both are
properties of a temperature \emph{step} rather than of a single operator, which
is why neither appears in Chapter~\ref{ch:theory}.

Let the plasma sit at the collisional--radiative equilibrium of
$\Lmat(\Te,\nel)$, and let the electron temperature step to $\Te+\dTe$ at
$t=0$ with $\nel$ held fixed. Write $\Lmat^-$ and $\Lmat^+$ for the operators
before and after, in the notation of Section~\ref{sec:two_clocks}. The
\emph{step error}
\begin{equation}
  \epsstep = \left|\frac{\Rcre(\Te,\nel)}
                        {\Rcre(\Te+\dTe,\nel)} - 1\right|
  \label{eq:eps_step_def}
\end{equation}

\noindent is the dimensionless distance between the two tabulated answers: what
the reading would change by if the plasma re-equilibrated instantly. The
\emph{plateau error}
\begin{equation}
  \epsplat = \left|\frac{\Rpe}
                        {\Rcre(\Te+\dTe,\nel)} - 1\right|,
  \qquad
  \Rpe \equiv \Rqss\!\left(\Te+\dTe,\nel;\,\bdep^-\right),
  \label{eq:eps_plateau_def}
\end{equation}

\noindent is the distance between the tabulated answer and the state the plasma
occupies once the excited manifold has settled but before the ground state has
moved: the post-step excited response of Eq.~\eqref{eq:qss_ratio}, evaluated on
the \emph{pre-step} reservoir. Both are dimensionless, both are non-negative,
and both are zero for a zero step.

Keeping them separate matters because they are not close. Across the operating
grid $\epsplat$ exceeds $\epsstep$ at every one of the 680 point-and-direction
pairs that possess a plateau, with a median ratio near $12$ and a minimum of
$1.45$ (\texttt{verify\_plateau\_gridmap.py}). A diagnostician who pictures the
reading sliding smoothly from one tabulated value to the other has not merely
misjudged the size of the excursion; they have the wrong shape of transient.

That comparison carries a caution about the ratio itself. Because
$\log(\epsplat/\epsstep) = \log\epsplat - \log\epsstep$ identically, any
correlation between the amplification and $\epsstep$ is partly tautological,
and the amplification blows up wherever $\epsstep$ passes through zero. The
apparent grid maximum of $1271\times$ sits at the point of smallest $\epsstep$
on the grid, where $\epsplat$ is itself below the median
(\texttt{verify\_plateau\_gridmap.py}). $\epsplat$ is the primary quantity
throughout this chapter and the amplification is reported only as a
distribution.

\textbf{Both quantities are defined by a step, so neither has a value until the
step is named.} $\Lmat$ is tabulated only on the temperature grid, so every
step used here is an integer number of grid intervals, and one interval is a
factor $1.048113$, that is $+\SI{4.81}{\percent}$ heating or
$-\SI{4.59}{\percent}$ cooling
(\texttt{validation/plateau\_gridmap/plateau\_gridmap.txt}, lines 10--11 and
18). The reader should read $\SI{4.81}{\percent}$ as an artifact of the choice
of 50 logarithmically spaced points between $1$ and \SI{10}{\electronvolt}, and
nothing else. Every $\epsplat$ quoted in this chapter carries the step that
produced it.

\subsection{The closure everyone worries about is not the dominant error}
\label{sec:qss_is_exact}

Before the maps, the negative result that reframes them.

$\epsQSS$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_qss} asks whether the excited states have kept up
with the reservoir they actually see. Integrating the full 43-state system
through the step and evaluating both errors along the same trajectory gives, at
the benchmark point, $\epsQSS = 8.66\times10^{-6}$ against
$\epsCRE = 6.34\times10^{-2}$; at $[0,4]$, the grid point carrying the largest
$\epsCRE$ anywhere, $\epsQSS = 6.73\times10^{-9}$ against
$\epsCRE = 3.869\times10^{-1}$ (\texttt{findings\_09}~\S1, from direct
integration).

At those two points the two errors differ by 3.9 and 7.8 decades, and they
differ most at the point where the diagnostic error is worst. Over the 392
analysed cells the gap runs from 2.9 to 9.7 decades
(Figure~\ref{fig:reversal}b), so it is a distribution rather than a single
number and should not be quoted as one. Neither figure is zero: the
excited-state closure is an approximation and it carries a measurable residual.
The point is its size relative to the other error, not its vanishing. The adiabatic elimination of
Section~\ref{sec:qss} is doing its job to a part in $10^{8}$ precisely where the
tabulated answer is off by \SI{39}{\percent} per $+\SI{4.81}{\percent}$
temperature step.

The reason is the spectrum. Section~\ref{sec:two_clocks} found one slow mode
and then a band, with nothing in between: at $\Lmat[1,4]$, the post-step
operator at the worst point, the three slowest eigenvalues are
$-4.29$, $-1.60\times10^{8}$ and $-3.44\times10^{8}\,\si{\per\second}$, a gap of
$3.7\times10^{7}$ (\texttt{findings\_10}~\S3.2). An excited population cannot
lag a reservoir that moves $10^{7}$ times more slowly than it can respond.

Figure~\ref{fig:reversal} puts both errors on the same axes over the whole
grid. The closure residual never exceeds $4.4\times10^{-5}$ anywhere on the
analysed grid; the distance to the tabulated equilibrium runs from
\SI{1.66}{\percent} to \SI{38.7}{\percent} over the same cells.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig5_5_reversal}
  \caption{\CapFigReversal}
  \label{fig:reversal}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The failure is therefore not in the elimination but in the closure
chosen for the eliminated variable.} A two-parameter table sets
$\bdep = \bdep^{\mathrm{CRE}}(\Te,\nel)$, which asserts that the plasma has
finished changing. The rest of this chapter is about what that assertion costs.

\subsection{The density structure is a broad crest, not a line}

Figure~\ref{fig:eps_vs_ne} cuts the map along the density axis. Every
temperature row has a single interior maximum in $\nel$: the error is small at
both ends of the density range and largest in between.
Section~\ref{sec:density_mechanism} explains why. What matters first is how
sharply that maximum is located, because the temptation is to read a ridge line
off a colour map and quote a density.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{fig5_2_eps_vs_ne}
  \caption[The density dependence, all eight columns]{\CapFigEpsVsNe}
  \label{fig:eps_vs_ne}
\end{figure}

It is not sharply located. Across the peak column and its two neighbours, a
factor $7.2$ in density, $\epsplat$ varies by only $6$ to
\SI{22}{\percent} depending on the row, while over the full three decades the
same row falls by a factor $2.2$ to $6.6$
(\texttt{make\_ch5\_figures.py}, injected into the caption of
Figure~\ref{fig:eps_vs_ne}). The crest is real and it is wide-topped. The grid
spacing is a factor $2.683$ per column, so nothing finer than that is resolved
in any case.

\textbf{This chapter therefore quotes the density of maximum error as the range
$7\times10^{12}$ to $\SI{5e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$}, which is the pair of
grid columns bracketing the modal maximum at
$\SI{1.93e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, and not as a single value. Two further
facts support the range and neither supports a point value. The column at
$\SI{5.18e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ carries the row maximum in 3 of the 49
heating rows, all of them the coldest, and
$\SI{1.93e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ carries it in the rest; per-row argmax
over rows $0$ to $15$ is $[4,4,4,3,3,3,3,3,3,3,3,3,3,3,3,3]$
(\texttt{findings\_10}~\S1.2). And a parabolic fit in $\ln\nel$ through the
three columns around the maximum places its vertex at
$\SI{1.65e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, a \SI{15}{\percent} shift off the grid
node \emph{[UNVERIFIED: the parabolic vertex $1.65\times10^{13}$ is recorded in
\texttt{findings\_10}~\S B.3 as prose only. No script in the repository
computes it and no artifact under \texttt{validation/} stores it. What would
close this: a sub-grid fit written into
\texttt{verify\_eps\_gridmap.py} that stamps its vertex and residual to
\texttt{validation/}.]}

Reporting a single density would also overstate what this result is about. The
crest belongs to the $n=3$ and $n=4$ pair specifically. An
$H_\alpha/H_\gamma$ diagnostic, which uses $n=3$ against $n=5$, has its worst
density at $\SI{7.2e12}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, a factor $2.68$ lower
(\texttt{findings\_10}~\S7.3). The correct form of words is that the
$H_\alpha/H_\beta$ ratio is least reliable near a certain density, never that
the Balmer diagnostic is.

There is no interior maximum in temperature anywhere in the defended range. The
error falls monotonically from \SI{18.07}{\percent} at
\SI{2.02}{\electronvolt} to \SI{4.55}{\percent} at \SI{8.29}{\electronvolt}
along the crest column, both per $+\SI{4.81}{\percent}$ step
(\texttt{verify\_ridge\_mechanism.py}). The apparent peak near
\SI{2}{\electronvolt} is the floor of the scope restriction of
Section~\ref{sec:scope}, not a feature of the plasma.

\section{Why a percentage is not a result}
\label{sec:step_convention}
% QUESTION: is the headline error a property of the plasma, or of the step size
% chosen to probe it?
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Every number in the previous section is attached to a $+\SI{4.81}{\percent}$
temperature step, and $\SI{4.81}{\percent}$ is not a physical quantity. It is
what one gets from 50 logarithmically spaced grid points between $1$ and
\SI{10}{\electronvolt}. An examiner is entitled to ask what happens for an ELM
that changes the temperature by \SI{10}{\percent}, and a chapter whose headline
is a percentage has no answer.

The answer, over the grid steps available, is that the error grows fast:

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Grid maximum of $\epsplat$ against the size of the imposed temperature
step, in grid intervals. Values from \texttt{verify\_eps\_gridmap.py} via
\texttt{CH5\_EVIDENCE.md}~\S1; the one-interval and four-interval maxima sit at
$[0,4]$ and $[0,5]$ respectively, both on the cold edge, and neither is
quantitative (Section~\ref{sec:scope}). The middle row is recorded in
\texttt{pivot\_decision.md}~\S2 without a location.}
\label{tab:step_dependence}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
  \toprule
  step (grid intervals) & step in $\Te$ & grid maximum of $\epsplat$ \\
  \midrule
  1 & $+\SI{4.81}{\percent}$  & \SI{38.7}{\percent} \\
  2 & $+\SI{9.85}{\percent}$  & \SI{87.4}{\percent} \\
  4 & $+\SI{20.68}{\percent}$ & \SI{190.4}{\percent} \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

A factor $4.3$ in step size moves the reported error by a factor $4.9$. Quoted
bare, \SI{38.7}{\percent} is a statement about a grid, and the sentence
\emph{``the quasi-steady-state error reaches \SI{39}{\percent}''} is not
falsifiable because it does not say what it is \SI{39}{\percent} of.

The way out is not a better choice of step. It is to report the response
function instead of one of its values, which is what
Section~\ref{sec:exact_decomposition} does. That the error more than doubles
between the first and second rows of Table~\ref{tab:step_dependence}, while the
quantity introduced there moves by \SI{2}{\percent}, is the reason for
preferring the second object to the first.

\section{The exact response to a finite step}

\subsection{The split has exactly two channels}
\label{sec:slow_subspace}

Everything in this section and the two that follow rests on a partition made in
Chapter~\ref{ch:theory}: one slow state, the ground state $1s$, and $42$ fast
ones. That partition deserves an argument rather than an assertion, because
there is an obvious candidate for a second slow state.

The $2s$ level cannot decay to the ground state by emitting one dipole photon,
because both states have zero orbital angular momentum and a single dipole
photon must carry one unit of it away. It decays instead by emitting two
photons at once, at $8.229\,\si{\per\second}$, a lifetime of
\SI{0.12}{\second} against roughly a nanosecond for the $2p$ level beside it.
If $2s$ held population on the slow timescale it would be a second reservoir:
the supply sum $\nF = \bm{a}\,\ngs + \bm{c}\,\nion$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:two_supplies} would gain a third term, the logistic of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:f_logistic} would not merely be displaced but would change shape,
and the bound of Section~\ref{sec:bound} would not follow.

In a plasma $2s$ is not depopulated by radiation. Proton impact mixes it into
$2p$, which then decays promptly, and that route carries between \SI{99.92}{\percent}
and \SI{99.99}{\percent} of the $2s$ loss budget across the grid, the floor
falling at \SI{10}{\electronvolt} and the ceiling at the cold edge
(\texttt{make\_story\_figures.py}). At the thinnest and coldest point on the grid,
$\Te = \SI{1}{\electronvolt}$, $\nel = \SI{e12}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, the
total $2s$ loss rate is
$|L_{2s,2s}| = 1.2564\times10^{9}\,\si{\per\second}$ against a slow
eigenvalue $|\lambda_0| = 0.0149\,\si{\per\second}$, a separation of
$8\times10^{10}$.

That argument leans on a proton-impact rate coefficient, so it is worth having
one that does not. Delete the $\ell$-mixing entirely and leave $2s$ with
nothing but two-photon emission: $8.229\,\si{\per\second}$ is still $553$
times faster than $|\lambda_0|$ at the same corner. \textbf{On this grid $2s$
cannot be slow under any assumption about $\ell$-mixing whatever.} The density
at which it would become a second reservoir is near
$\SI{7e3}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, $8.2$ decades below the bottom of the
operating range.

The direct spectral test agrees and supplies the number to quote. Over all
$400$ grid points the smallest ratio of $|\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda|$ over the
excited block $\Lmat_{FF}$ to $|\lambda_0|$ is $86.5$, at
$\Te = \SI{10}{\electronvolt}$, $\nel = \SI{e15}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$,
and no slow eigenvector anywhere on the grid carries dominant $2s$ weight.
There is no near-degenerate second slow mode. That point and that value are the
same stress point and essentially the same quantity as the timescale-separation
minimum $\Msep = 86.8$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:M_range}, computed from the excited
block rather than from the full matrix, so the two are a consistency check on
each other and not two results. \textbf{The worst-case separation is $86.5$,
and that is the figure that describes the grid}; the benchmark value
$\Msep = 9982$ describes one point and overstates the margin by two orders of
magnitude.

submission; no other statement in this chapter depends on them.]}

\subsection{A better variable than the departure coefficient}

Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} worked in $\bdep = \ngs/(\Zsb{1}\nel\nion)$, because
that is the variable in which the observable's response has the closed form of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:R_of_b}. Here it is better to use the reservoir itself,
\begin{equation}
  u \;\equiv\; \frac{\ngs}{\nion} \;=\; \bdep\,\Zsb{1}(\Te)\,\nel ,
  \label{eq:u_def}
\end{equation}

\noindent a dimensionless ground-state population per unit ion density. At
fixed $(\Te,\nel)$ the two are proportional, so $\mathrm{d}\ln u =
\mathrm{d}\ln\bdep$ and the sensitivity of
Section~\ref{sec:ground_fed_fraction} carries over unchanged. The reason for
the change of variable is that a temperature step moves $\Zsb{1}$: comparing a
pre-step $\bdep$ with a post-step one would mix a change in the reservoir with a
change in the Saha--Boltzmann normalisation, and $u$ has no normalisation to
move. That confusion is not hypothetical. An earlier version of the analysis
reported in this section made exactly it, and the resulting contamination in
$\Delta\ln\bdep$ was comparable to the quantity being measured.

\textbf{Assumption.} Equation~\eqref{eq:u_def} treats $\nion$ as a fixed
reservoir, unchanged by the step, as throughout this thesis
(Section~\ref{sec:rate_equation}). This removes the freedom for the ionisation
balance to respond, and it is what makes $u$ numerically equal to the
ground-state population in the solved system. Chapter~\ref{ch:limits} returns to
what a mobile ion population would change, and bounds it: the total bound
population per unit $\nion$ at the benchmark is $8.925\times10^{-4}$, so
freezing $\nion$ costs at most \SI{0.09}{\percent}
(\texttt{findings\_10}~\S B.4).

\subsection{The decomposition}

Equation~\eqref{eq:R_of_b} makes the post-step observable a ratio of two affine
functions of $u$, so the logarithmic sensitivity of
Section~\ref{sec:ground_fed_fraction} is $\mathrm{d}\ln\Robs/\mathrm{d}\ln u =
\ffrac{3}-\ffrac{4}$, the difference of the two ground-fed fractions.
Integrating between the frozen and the equilibrated reservoir gives, with no
approximation,
\begin{equation}
  \ln\frac{\Rpe}{\Rcre}
  \;=\; \int_{\ln u^+}^{\ln u^-}\!\!\big(\ffrac{3}-\ffrac{4}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\ln u
  \;=\; \overline{S}\;\Delta\ln u ,
  \label{eq:exact_decomposition}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\Delta\ln u = \ln(u^-/u^+)$ is the reservoir displacement and
\begin{equation}
  \overline{S} \;\equiv\;
  \frac{1}{\Delta\ln u}\int_{\ln u^+}^{\ln u^-}\!\!
  \big(\ffrac{3}-\ffrac{4}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\ln u
  \label{eq:Sbar_def}
\end{equation}

\noindent is the mean sensitivity over the interval the reservoir traverses.
Both factors are dimensionless. Since $\ffrac{3},\ffrac{4}\in[0,1]$ by
Section~\ref{sec:ground_fed_fraction}, and $\overline{S}$ is an average of their
difference, $|\overline{S}|$ is bounded by the peak sensitivity of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:peak_sensitivity} and so is strictly less than unity everywhere.
Exponentiating gives the plateau error:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\ \epsplat = \left|\,\mathrm{e}^{\overline{S}\,\Delta\ln u} - 1\,\right|\ }
  \label{eq:eps_plateau_exact}
\end{equation}

A single scalar error has become a product of two quantities that can be
measured independently: how strongly the observable responds to its reservoir,
and how far the reservoir moved. Nothing in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact_decomposition} says which of them varies across the
operating map, and Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} determines that.

\subsection{The reservoir gain, and why it is the step-independent object}

The second factor still contains the step. Splitting it,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\ln u \;=\; G\,\Delta\ln\Te ,
  \qquad
  G \;\equiv\; \frac{\mathrm{d}\ln u}{\mathrm{d}\ln\Te}
  \;=\; \frac{\partial \ln u^{\mathrm{CRE}}}{\partial\ln\Te}\bigg|_{\nel},
  \label{eq:gain_def}
\end{equation}

\noindent defines the \emph{reservoir gain} $G$: the factor by which a
fractional change in electron temperature is amplified into a fractional change
in the equilibrium ground-state population. It is dimensionless, it is negative
throughout the grid because heating ionises the ground state away, and unlike
$\epsplat$ it is a property of the operating point rather than of the
experiment done to it. Substituting into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_plateau_exact},
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\ \epsplat = \Big|\exp\!\big(\overline{S}\,G\,\Delta\ln\Te\big)-1\Big|\ },
  \qquad
  \lim_{\Delta\ln\Te\to0}\frac{\mathrm{d}\,\epsplat}{\mathrm{d}\ln\Te}
  = \big|\overline{S}\,G\big| .
  \label{eq:eps_of_step}
\end{equation}

\noindent The small-step limit on the right is the useful form: it is the
diagnostic error per unit fractional temperature excursion, with the excursion
divided out. At the benchmark point it is $1.31$, at $[15,3]$ it is $3.54$ and
at $[0,4]$ it is $6.96$, all computed as $|\overline{S}G|$ from the values in
Table~\ref{tab:gain}. Read the middle one as: in the worst conditions this
thesis defends, a \SI{1}{\percent} temperature transient costs the Balmer
inversion about \SI{3.5}{\percent} in the line ratio.

\subsection{A worked number at the benchmark}

The chain closes arithmetically. At $[23,5]$ the mean sensitivity over the step
is $|\overline{S}| = 0.2288$ (\texttt{findings\_10} ADDENDUM~B.2, which lists it
alongside $\Delta = 1.9403$ and $\tanh(\Delta/4) = 0.4503$ at that point), the
gain is $G = -5.736$, and one grid interval is $\Delta\ln\Te = 0.046984$. Then
\begin{equation*}
  \overline{S}\,G\,\Delta\ln\Te = 0.2288 \times 5.736 \times 0.046984 = 0.06166,
  \qquad
  \mathrm{e}^{0.06166} - 1 = 0.0636 ,
\end{equation*}
%
against the $0.063612$ that \texttt{verify\_plateau\_gridmap.py} obtains for the
same point by an exact linear solve. Three quantities from three different
artifacts multiply to the recorded error to four significant figures. The
arithmetic is performed here in the text and is not itself a run.

\subsection{Use the exact form, not the linearisation}

The first-order form $\epsplat \approx |\overline{S}\,\Delta\ln u|$ is what one
would write down first, and it is adequate for a small step and wrong for a
large one. At the four-interval step it returns \SI{59.4}{\percent} where
Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_plateau_exact} gives \SI{81.1}{\percent} at $[15,3]$
(\texttt{verify\_ridge\_mechanism.py}). The exponential costs nothing and this
chapter uses it throughout.

It would be convenient if the linearisation erred in one direction only, so
that it could be quoted as a bound. It does not, and an earlier draft of this
work claimed that it did. Which way it errs depends on which linearisation is
meant. For the \emph{endpoint} form actually computed at
\texttt{verify\_plateau\_gridmap.py}, line 212, the ratio of measured to
predicted error over the heating map runs $0.8417$ to $1.3891$ with median
$1.0310$: the linearised expression understates in 219 of 392 heating steps and
overstates in the rest, and at the benchmark it overstates, with ratio $0.9459$
(\texttt{findings\_10}~\S B.3). \textbf{Any statement about the linearisation
must name which linearisation it is about.}

A final caution about what the decomposition can and cannot be used for.
Equation~\eqref{eq:exact_decomposition} is an identity, so comparing its two
sides tests nothing: a check that $\epsplat$ equals
$|\mathrm{e}^{\overline{S}\Delta\ln u}-1|$ can fail only through arithmetic
error. What the identity permits is an \emph{attribution}. The two factors are
separately measurable, so one may ask which of them carries the structure the
maps show. That is a weaker kind of statement than a derivation, and it is the
kind made in Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}.

\section{A bound that no atomic dataset can exceed}
\label{sec:bound}
% QUESTION: is there a limit on the diagnostic error that holds independently of
% the rate coefficients, and what exactly does it constrain?
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Section~\ref{sec:ground_fed_fraction} showed that in the logarithmic reservoir
variable both ground-fed fractions are the same unit-width logistic curve,
displaced by $\Delta = \ln[(a_3/a_4)/(c_3/c_4)]$, and that their difference
therefore has the closed-form maximum
$\max|\ffrac{3}-\ffrac{4}| = \tanh(|\Delta|/4)$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:peak_sensitivity}. Since $\overline{S}$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:Sbar_def} is an average of $\ffrac{3}-\ffrac{4}$ over an
interval, it inherits the bound:
\begin{equation}
  \big|\overline{S}\big| \;\le\; \tanh\!\left(\frac{|\Delta|}{4}\right) \;<\; 1 .
  \label{eq:Sbar_bound}
\end{equation}

Three properties make this worth stating separately from the algebra that
produced it.

\textbf{It does not depend on the atomic data.} The $\tanh$ is a consequence of
the logistic having unit width, and the width is unity because the excited
populations are exactly affine in $\ngs$, which is a structural property of the
linear system and not of any rate coefficient
(\texttt{findings\_10} ADDENDUM~B.1). Every property of the 42-state network
enters only through where the two curves sit, that is through $\Delta$.
Consistent with that, $\Delta$ is invariant under rescaling the $\ngs/\nion$
normalisation, verified exactly under factors of $10^{10}$ and $10^{-7}$, and
is insensitive to the $\ell$-weighting used to bundle a shell: population sum,
$p$-states, $d$, $s$ and statistically weighted give $1.94561$, $1.94474$,
$1.94447$, $1.94471$ and $1.94574$ at the benchmark, a spread of
\SI{0.07}{\percent} (\texttt{findings\_10}~\S B.5). The individual switching
points $x_3$ and $x_4$ and the fractions $\ffrac{p}$ are \emph{not} invariant
under that rescaling; only their separation is.

\textbf{Its physical content is a statement about amplification.} Because
$|\mathrm{d}\ln\Robs/\mathrm{d}\ln\bdep| < 1$ everywhere, integrating over any
excursion gives $|\Delta\ln\Robs| \le |\Delta\ln\bdep|$. A line ratio can never
respond to the ground-state reservoir faster, in relative terms, than the
reservoir itself moves. A stale reservoir cannot be amplified in logarithmic
space, only attenuated.

\textbf{The bound is loose in general and tight at the crest.} At the benchmark
$|\overline{S}| = 0.2288$ against a cap of $0.4503$, so the sensitivity is at
\SI{51}{\percent} of what the atomic structure permits. At $[23,3]$, one column
into the crest, $|\overline{S}| = 0.4261$ against a cap of $0.4394$, that is
\SI{97}{\percent} (both from the table in \texttt{findings\_10}~\S B.2;
the percentages are arithmetic on those two columns). Where the error is
largest, the diagnostic is running at essentially its structural maximum
sensitivity to the reservoir, and no refinement of the rate coefficients can
make that worse.

\subsection{What the bound does not constrain}
\label{sec:inversion_jacobian}

A diagnostician does not suffer an error in $\Robs$. They measure $\Robs$ and
invert it for $(\Te,\nel)$, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:inversion_jacobian} makes the
inferred temperature error
$\Delta\ln\Te = (\mathrm{d}\ln\Robs/\mathrm{d}\ln\bdep)/
(\mathrm{d}\ln\Robs/\mathrm{d}\ln\Te)\,\Delta\ln\bdep$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:Sbar_bound} constrains the numerator. It says nothing about
the denominator, and Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} deferred the denominator to this
chapter.

The denominator vanishes. Scanning $\Te$ at
$\nel = \SI{5.18e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, $\epsstep$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_step_def} passes through a minimum of $4.9\times10^{-5}$ near
$\Te = \SI{6.87}{\electronvolt}$, with values $7.18\times10^{-4}$,
$3.79\times10^{-4}$, $4.9\times10^{-5}$, $2.74\times10^{-4}$ and
$5.89\times10^{-4}$ at $6.251$, $6.551$, $6.866$, $7.197$ and
\SI{7.543}{\electronvolt} (\texttt{verify\_plateau\_gridmap.py}). Since
$\epsstep$ is the fractional distance between the two tabulated answers either
side of the step, a zero means $\Rcre$ is stationary in temperature there:
$\partial\ln\Robs/\partial\ln\Te = 0$.

\textbf{The $n=3/n=4$ ratio is locally non-invertible for temperature near
\SI{6.9}{\electronvolt} at this density.} There, a bounded error in the
observable produces an unbounded error in the inferred temperature, and the
plateau error is not the relevant failure mode at all: the diagnostic has no
temperature resolution to lose. This is a separate failure from the one the
rest of the chapter maps, it is arguably the more severe of the two for a
practitioner, and it is visible only because $\epsstep$ was computed and kept
rather than discarded as uninteresting.

The scan above is one density column. \emph{[UNVERIFIED: whether the locus
$\partial\ln\Robs/\partial\ln\Te = 0$ crosses the operating range at other
densities has not been mapped. What would close this: evaluate $\epsstep$ on
the full grid, which \texttt{verify\_plateau\_gridmap.py} already does, and
contour its minimum.]}

\section{Where the error is largest, and why}
\label{sec:mechanism}
% QUESTION: which physical quantity puts the maximum of the error map where it
% is, and is it the same quantity on both axes?
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The maps of Section~\ref{sec:error_maps} show where the diagnostic error is
large. They do not say why, and a map without a mechanism is a lookup table
that cannot be extrapolated past the grid that produced it.

The answer has two halves and they disagree. The structure along the density
axis and the structure along the temperature axis have different causes, and
reading them as one effect, which the appearance of a single crested surface
invites, gets both wrong.

\subsection{Across density: the operating point, not the span}
\label{sec:density_mechanism}

Evaluating both factors of Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact_decomposition} at every grid
point settles which of them locates the crest
(\texttt{verify\_ridge\_mechanism.py}, over $\Te = 2$--\SI{9}{\electronvolt}
and all eight density columns). The column maximising $\epsplat$ is the column
maximising $|\overline{S}|$ in $32$ of $32$ temperature rows for a one-interval
step and $31$ of $31$ for a four-interval step; the column maximising
$|\Delta\ln u|$ coincides with the error maximum in $0$ of $32$ and $0$ of $31$.

Coincidence counts are weak evidence on their own, because a quantity that
barely varies still has its maximum somewhere. The amplitudes settle it. Across
the three decades of density $|\overline{S}|$ varies by a factor $7.11$ and
$|\Delta\ln u|$ by a factor $1.01$. \textbf{A quantity that changes by one per
cent cannot produce a crest in which the error changes by a factor of seven},
wherever its own maximum falls.

That settles \emph{which factor}. It does not settle why that factor has an
interior maximum, and here the obvious explanation is wrong.
Equation~\eqref{eq:Sbar_bound} caps the sensitivity at $\tanh(|\Delta|/4)$, so
the natural guess is that $\Delta$ itself peaks near
$\SI{2e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$: that the two supply channels are most
widely separated there, and the sensitivity follows. That guess was written
down in advance by an independent re-derivation of Chapter~\ref{ch:theory},
together with a structural argument for why $\Delta$ should collapse at high
density, namely that in local thermodynamic equilibrium (LTE) both channels
would give the same Boltzmann shape and $\Delta$ would go to zero.

\textbf{It is falsified.} $\Delta$ is nearly flat across the density axis:
$0.9655$ at $\SI{e12}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, $1.9403$ at

$\SI{7e12}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ the cap $\tanh(|\Delta|/4)$ spans only
$0.394$ to $0.450$, a \SI{14}{\percent} range. The grid never reaches LTE, and
because the model is open, with $\Svec_F$ an externally imposed source not tied
to $\nion$ by the Saha relation, the two channels cannot merge by construction
(\texttt{findings\_10} ADDENDUM~B.2).

puts the two side by side: while the cap moves by \SI{14}{\percent}, the
position of the CRE reservoir relative to the sensitivity peak,

\textbf{The maximum sits where $\ln(u^{\mathrm{CRE}}/u^{\mathrm{peak}})$ changes
sign.} The physical reading is direct. Both $\ffrac{3}$ and $\ffrac{4}$ rise
from zero to unity as the reservoir grows, and they switch from
recombination-fed to ground-fed at different reservoir strengths, so their
difference vanishes in both supply limits and peaks between them. The
sensitivity curve is fixed. Raising the density slides the plasma's own CRE
reservoir along it, from the ground-fed side at low density, through the
mixed-supply region, to the recombination-fed side at high density. The crest
is where that slide crosses the peak.

Two consequences follow, and both matter. First, this is a statement about
where the operating point sits, not about how sensitive the diagnostic could
be, and it replaces the weaker description of the density axis as
sensitivity-dominated: both are true, but only the first says why. Second, it
forbids the inference that the effect disappears at high density. The cap does
not close; the plasma merely walks off the peak. On a grid extending further in
density it would walk back on.

\subsection{Three independent tests of the mechanism}
\label{sec:griem_tests}

The account above predicts more than the shape of one curve, and each
prediction can fail.

\textbf{The crest must move with the shell pair, and by a predicted amount.}
The reservoir strength at which a shell switches from recombination-fed to
ground-fed is a version of Griem's LTE
criterion~\cite{Griem1963,Griem1997}, which places the boundary between
collisionally coupled and radiatively decoupled levels at a density scaling as
$n^{-17/2}$ in the principal quantum number. Moving the diagnostic from the
$(3,4)$ pair to the $(4,5)$ pair should therefore move the crest by
$(5/4)^{8.5} = 6.7$. Measured: $7.2$. The argmax of $|\ffrac{n}-\ffrac{n+1}|$
at $\Te = \SI{2.02}{\electronvolt}$ runs $\SI{3.73e14}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$
for $(2,3)$, $\SI{1.93e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ for $(3,4)$ and
$\SI{2.68e12}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ for $(4,5)$, with $(5,6)$ and above
falling off the bottom of the grid (\texttt{verify\_ridge\_mechanism.py}).

\textbf{The absolute density must match, not just the ratio.} Griem's LTE
criterion evaluated at $n=4$ and $\Te = \SI{2}{\electronvolt}$ gives
$\SI{2.05e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ against a measured crest at
$\SI{1.93e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, a \SI{6}{\percent} difference.

\textbf{An independent diagnostic must put the boundary level in the same
place.} \texttt{verify\_boundary\_descent.py} computes, without reference to
$\ffrac{3}$ or $\ffrac{4}$, the effective principal quantum number at which the
collisional and radiative rates balance. At the crest density it returns
$\bar n = 3.08$ to $3.25$ at every temperature on the grid
(\texttt{validation/boundary\_descent.csv}), that is, between $n=3$ and $n=4$,
which is exactly the pair whose supply fractions differ most.

\textbf{What would have refuted the mechanism, and did not occur:} the crest
failing to move with the shell pair, or moving the wrong way. A competing
explanation was refuted outright. The suggestion that both shells become
ground-fed at high density because collisional coupling dominates predicts
$\ffrac{3},\ffrac{4}\to1$; measured at $\Te = \benchTe$,
$\nel = \SI{e15}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, they are $0.071$ and $0.011$, so
both shells become \emph{continuum}-fed instead
(\texttt{findings\_10}~\S2).

\subsection{Alternatives that would produce the same appearance}
\label{sec:ridge_alternatives}

An interior maximum on a coarse grid can be manufactured in several ways, and
the attribution above does not by itself exclude them.

\emph{Grid resolution.} The crest occupies one density column and the grid is
spaced by a factor $2.683$, so its location is known only to that factor. What
is established is that the maximum is interior, not at either end of the
density range, and that it does not move between temperature rows above
\SI{1.15}{\electronvolt}. Refining the density grid requires rebuilding the
rate matrix and revalidating every number in Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} against
the new one; future work could determine the location more precisely.

\emph{Sub-grid drift.} A parabolic fit in $\ln\nel$ puts the peak at
$\SI{3.66e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ at \SI{1}{\electronvolt} and
$\SI{1.51e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ at \SI{9.5}{\electronvolt}, a range of
$2.56\times$, which drifts \emph{downward} with temperature while Griem's
criterion predicts an upward drift as $\sqrt{\Te}$, a factor $3.2$
(\texttt{findings\_10}~\S7.2). The whole drift is smaller than one grid
interval and therefore unresolved, so the crest should be described as constant
to within a factor $2.68$ over $1$ to \SI{10}{\electronvolt}, never as fixed.
The likely reason for the sign is that the neutral fraction $\ngs/\nion$
collapses across the grid, dragging the supply crossover down in density faster
than the Griem boundary drags it up, which is consistent with
Section~\ref{sec:density_mechanism} but has not been separated from it.

\emph{Step-size convention.} A crest whose position depended on the size of the
imposed step would be an artifact of that choice. It does not: the error
maximum falls on the same density column for a one-interval and a four-interval
step in $31$ of the $31$ temperature rows both can reach, and so does the
maximum of $|\overline{S}|$ (\texttt{verify\_ridge\_mechanism.py}). This tests
steps of fixed \emph{fractional} size; a step of fixed absolute size in
electron-volts spans a different number of grid intervals at each temperature
and is a different experiment, not tested here.

\emph{Truncation.} The model resolves $n\le8$ and bundles $n=9$--$15$. The
mechanism turns on the difference between the $n=3$ and $n=4$ supply fractions,
both fully resolved, but those fractions depend on the recombination flux
cascading from above, which the bundling approximates. \emph{[UNVERIFIED: the
sensitivity of the crest to $n_{\max}$ has not been tested.
\texttt{src/validation/verify\_bundling\_psm20.py} exists for this purpose and
has never been run.]}

\emph{The population set.} Two of the scripts used here apply different filters:
\texttt{verify\_plateau\_gridmap.py} excludes the $54$ points with no plateau
window, and \texttt{verify\_ridge\_mechanism.py} does not. All $54$ lie at
$j\ge4$, so the ridge script's statistics for the two highest density columns
include points at which the plateau state being computed does not physically
exist (\texttt{findings\_10}~\S B.3). The attribution of
Section~\ref{sec:density_mechanism} does not depend on those columns, but a
reader recomputing the profile from the raw output should apply the filter.

\subsection{Across temperature: a displacement effect}
\label{sec:temperature_mechanism}

The temperature dependence has the opposite explanation, and this is where
treating the two axes alike would go wrong.

Along the crest column the plateau error falls monotonically from
\SI{18.07}{\percent} at $\Te = \SI{2.02}{\electronvolt}$ to \SI{4.55}{\percent}
at \SI{8.29}{\electronvolt}, a factor $3.72$, both per $+\SI{4.81}{\percent}$
step. Decomposed through Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact_decomposition}, $|\Delta\ln u|$
contributes a factor $2.72$ of that fall and $|\overline{S}|$ only $1.29$; in
logarithmic terms the displacement carries \SI{76}{\percent} of the decay and
the sensitivity \SI{19}{\percent}. At the four-interval step the split is
\SI{72}{\percent} and \SI{13}{\percent}
(\texttt{verify\_ridge\_mechanism.py}).

The observable therefore does not become much less sensitive as the plasma
heats. \textbf{The ground state simply moves less.} In the language of
Section~\ref{sec:exact_decomposition}, the reservoir gain $|G|$ falls with
temperature, from $14.4$ at \SI{1.00}{\electronvolt} to $5.7$ at
\SI{2.95}{\electronvolt} (Table~\ref{tab:gain}), and a step of fixed fractional
size therefore displaces the reservoir progressively less.

Why the gain behaves this way is not established here. $|\Delta\ln u|$ is flat
to one per cent across three decades of density while varying by a factor $2.7$
across temperature, which implies that
$\partial\ln\ngs/\partial\ln\Te$ at collisional--radiative equilibrium is
controlled by temperature alone. \emph{[MECHANISM NOT ESTABLISHED: this is an
observation from \texttt{verify\_ridge\_mechanism.py}, not a derived result. A
natural candidate is the Arrhenius factor in the electron-impact ionisation
rate out of the ground state, whose logarithmic temperature derivative is
$\chi_H/\Te = 4.6$ at the benchmark against a measured $|G| = 5.7$, but the
comparison has not been carried through and the two need not be equal.]}

\subsection{Three explanations this work does not support}
\label{sec:withdrawn}

Three readings of the crest are natural and do not survive the numbers. Each is
recorded because it is among the first a reader is likely to propose, and one
of them was asserted in an earlier draft of this thesis.

\textbf{It is not detachment.} The crest sits at
$\SI{1.93e19}{\per\cubic\metre}$: a factor $5.2$ below the density band that
published edge modelling associates with a detached ITER
divertor~\cite{Guillemaut2011}, and a factor $1.6$ below the modelled upstream
separatrix density~\cite{Stangeby2023}. It falls between the two, in a region
neither source characterises. Two further facts make the identification
untenable rather than merely unsupported. At the citable detached band,
$\nel = 10^{14}$ to $\SI{e15}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ with
$\Te\ge\SI{2}{\electronvolt}$, the plateau error is at most \SI{10.4}{\percent}
per $+\SI{4.81}{\percent}$ step and the ELM-averaged bound of
Section~\ref{sec:persistence} never reaches \SI{10}{\percent}, peaking at
\SI{7.2}{\percent}. And the crest location is proportional to the assumed
neutral density, which in this model is set by a local ionisation balance with
no transport and no recycling, whereas in a divertor it is set by recycling;
Chapter~\ref{ch:limits} measures that proportionality and finds one decade in
$\ngs$ moves the crest by roughly one decade in $\nel$. The crest is
reproducible. Its identification with a named divertor operating regime is not
supported by anything in this work.

\textbf{It is not a peak in temperature.} The error decreases monotonically with
temperature throughout the defended range, as
Section~\ref{sec:temperature_mechanism} shows. The apparent ridge line at
\SI{2}{\electronvolt} in Figure~\ref{fig:eps_map} is the edge of the scope
restriction.

\textbf{It is not simply where $\bdep^{\mathrm{CRE}}$ meets the sensitivity
maximum.} That reading accounts for the density structure but fails on the
temperature axis: at the four-interval step the ratio
$\bdep^{\mathrm{CRE}}/\bdep^{\mathrm{peak}}$ passes through unity at
$\Te = \SI{4.7}{\electronvolt}$, where the error is \SI{33}{\percent} and
falling monotonically (\texttt{verify\_plateau\_gridmap.py}). The endpoint of
the reservoir trajectory is the wrong object: Eq.~\eqref{eq:Sbar_def} shows
that what enters is the mean sensitivity over the whole interval traversed.

\section{Does the transient visit the plateau?}
\label{sec:bridge}
% QUESTION: the partial-equilibrium state was constructed by freezing the ground
% state by hand. Does the time-dependent system actually pass through it?
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Everything so far is algebraic. $\Rpe$ was built by holding $\bdep$ at its
pre-step value and solving the post-step excited block, which is a construction,
not an observation. If the real trajectory never lingers near that state, the
map measures a fiction.

It does linger. Propagating the full 43-state system through the step with
\texttt{scipy.linalg.expm} and tracking $\epsCRE(t)$
(\texttt{verify\_plateau\_bridge.py}) gives, at the benchmark point and at
$[15,3]$:

\begin{itemize}
  \item The excited manifold reaches its instantaneous QSS state after
    $1.94\,\taurel$ at the benchmark and $3.72\,\taurel$ at $[15,3]$.
  \item Once settled, the state deviation from QSS is $1.3\times10^{-6}$ and
    $8.1\times10^{-8}$ respectively. These are the settled values, not the
    maxima over the plateau interval, which are $471$ and $1721$ times larger
    because the interval begins in the tail of the fast transient. An external
    review of this work quoted the maxima; the settled column is the one that
    supports the claim that the manifold has equilibrated.
  \item Over the plateau, $\epsCRE(t)$ departs from the analytic $\epsplat$ by
    \SI{3.33}{\percent} at the benchmark and \SI{1.28}{\percent} at $[15,3]$.
    That flatness is what licenses quoting a single $\epsplat$ instead of a
    time-dependent error, and it is measured rather than assumed.
\end{itemize}

The propagator itself was checked by running \texttt{expm} against
\texttt{expm\_multiply}, two independent algorithms, which agree to
$1.13\times10^{-10}$ and $3.69\times10^{-12}$. Two other checks that were run
are \emph{not} evidence and are recorded here so that nobody re-runs them: the
ODE residual is identically zero by construction, since
$\mathrm{d}\nvec/\mathrm{d}t$ and $\Lmat^+\nvec + \Svec^+$ are the same
expression evaluated twice, and the semigroup check is near-tautological for a
scaling-and-squaring implementation of \texttt{expm}, which is why it returned
exactly zero at both points.

\textbf{Scope.} This establishes the dynamic chain
$\text{full transient}\to\Rqss(u(t))\to\Rpe$ at two grid points, not grid-wide.
The density structure of Section~\ref{sec:density_mechanism} comes from a
grid-wide algebraic calculation. A grid-wide dynamic claim would need the bridge
test run at every point, which has not been done.

One honest record of a failed preregistration belongs here. The first version
of the plateau criterion required
$\epsQSS < 2\times10^{-3}$ throughout $20\,\taurel$ to $0.02\,\tauslow$, and it
\emph{failed} at $[15,3]$, reaching $2.778\times10^{-3}$ at the very end of the
window. The criterion was retained unchanged and the window was not shortened.
It failed because the ground state had begun its expected motion while the
tracking error stayed at $6.8\times10^{-7}$: the system had not left the QSS
manifold, it had moved \emph{along} it. The replacement interval definition,
used above, was not preregistered and is not described as such.

\section{How long the error lasts, and what an ELM averages}
\label{sec:persistence}
% QUESTION: does the plateau error survive averaging over a transient event of
% finite duration, or does it decay away inside the integration time?
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

A spectrometer integrates. If the error decayed on nanoseconds it would be
invisible in a \SI{100}{\micro\second} exposure, so the decay law matters as
much as the amplitude.

The error decays as a single exponential with time constant $\tauslow$. Fitting
$\epsCRE(t)$ over the decay gives $\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}/\tauslow = 1.0102$ at the
benchmark and $1.0656$ at $[15,3]$, with half-decay at $0.7002$ and
$0.7386\,\tauslow$ against $\ln 2 = 0.6931$ for a pure exponential
(\texttt{verify\_plateau\_bridge.py}). In physical units the half-life is
\SI{12.95}{\micro\second} at the benchmark and \SI{1.10}{\milli\second} at
$[15,3]$. Unlike the plateau duration, which is set by whatever tolerance
defines the end of the plateau, the half-life carries no threshold dependence.

That gives a time-average over an event of duration $\taudrive$:
\begin{equation}
  \big\langle\epsCRE\big\rangle_{\taudrive}
  \;\ge\; \epsplat\,\frac{\tauslow}{\taudrive}
          \left(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\taudrive/\tauslow}\right) ,
  \label{eq:elm_bound}
\end{equation}

\noindent which tends to $\epsplat$ as $\taudrive\to0$ and to zero as
$\taudrive\to\infty$, both as they should. Equation~\eqref{eq:elm_bound} was
constructed as a conservative lower bound, on the reasoning that the observable
cannot decay faster than the slowest mode. Direct 43-state eigen-propagation
shows it is much better than that: the ratio of the true \SI{100}{\micro\second}
average to the bound is between $0.9999$ and $1.0066$ at seven points including
the worst on the grid (\texttt{findings\_10}~\S3.2). The reason is the spectral
gap of Section~\ref{sec:qss_is_exact}. With nothing between $\lambda_0$ and
$\lambda_1$, there is no intermediate mode for the observable to decay on, so
the bound is essentially the answer. It should be described as a near-exact
estimate whose error is under \SI{0.7}{\percent} at every point tested, not as a
loose bound.

\subsection{Two structural objections, tested}

The lower bound of Eq.~\eqref{eq:elm_bound} uses $\tauslow$, and $\tauslow$ in
this model is the relaxation time of an \emph{open} system: the ground state
loses atoms to an ion reservoir that is held fixed and never gives them back.
An artificially long $\tauslow$ would inflate every entry in
Table~\ref{tab:elm_census}. Rebuilding the operator with an exactly
particle-conserving 44th state and recomputing the whole census changes the
count from $202$ to $200$ and the worst case from
\SI{38.68}{\percent} to \SI{38.56}{\percent}; above \SI{2}{\electronvolt} it
changes nothing at all, $45$ of $448$ (\texttt{findings\_10}~\S3.1). The
closure factor on $\tauslow$ reaches $41$ only where $\tauslow$ already exceeds
the event duration by three orders of magnitude, and is $1.00$ wherever
$\tauslow$ is comparable to $\taudrive$, which is where it could matter.
Chapter~\ref{ch:limits} states the caveat that this closure adds an ion
reservoir without closing the electron balance and without adding transport.

The second objection is that a step is an idealisation and a real ELM has a
finite rise time. Any ramp with $\taurel \ll t_{\mathrm{ramp}} \ll \tauslow$
reaches the same plateau, because the fast manifold tracks the ramp adiabatically
while the slow reservoir cannot follow it at all. The relevant Damk\"ohler-like
ratio is $\tauslow/\taudrive$, which is $2332$ at $[0,4]$, and not
$\taurel/\taudrive \approx 10^{-5}$, which describes the suppression of the fast
excited-manifold error and has nothing to say about the plateau. The step
idealisation is safe, and it is safe for the second reason and not the first.

\section{Timescale separation does not predict it}
\label{sec:M_does_not_predict}
% QUESTION: does the standard validity criterion for the quasi-steady-state
% approximation, the timescale separation M, indicate where the diagnostic fails?
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

A modeller deciding whether to trust a QSS-based inversion reaches for
$\Msep = \tauslow/\taurel$, the stiffness ratio that licenses eliminating the
fast variables in the first place. Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} measured it from
$86.8$ to $1.73\times10^{9}$ across the grid. The natural expectation is that
the diagnostic is worst where $\Msep$ is smallest.

It is not, and $\Msep$ carries no information about the diagnostic error once
the operating point is accounted for.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{fig5_3_M_vs_eps}
  \caption[Timescale separation does not predict closure error]{\CapFigMvsEps}
  \label{fig:M_vs_eps}
\end{figure}

The two extrema make the point without statistics. The largest $\Msep$ on the
grid, $1.7\times10^{9}$, sits at $[0,0]$ and carries
$\epsplat = \SI{12.0}{\percent}$, which is the 75th percentile of the
distribution. The largest error, \SI{38.7}{\percent} at $[0,4]$, sits $52\times$
away in density at an $\Msep$ smaller by a factor $46$. \textbf{The maxima of
the two quantities are $52\times$ apart in density, and the error at the
timescale-separation maximum is a factor $3.2$ below the worst.} Where $\Msep$
is largest is not where the diagnostic fails.

The raw association looks strong and is a temperature proxy. Over the $680$
analysed pairs, $\mathrm{corr}(\log\Msep, \log\epsplat) = +0.76$. But
$\log\Msep$ is \SI{93}{\percent} explained by $(\log\Te,\log\nel)$ alone, and a
bare Arrhenius factor $\mathrm{e}^{\chi_H/\Te}$ built from the hydrogen
ionisation potential $\chi_H = \SI{13.6}{\electronvolt}$, which contains no
dynamics whatever, correlates with $\log\epsplat$ at $+0.71$
(\texttt{make\_ch5\_figures.py}). Both quantities rise steeply toward low
temperature for unrelated reasons, and that shared trend is nearly all of the
$+0.76$.

Removing the shared trend reverses the sign. Residualising both $\log\Msep$ and
$\log\epsplat$ on $(\log\Te,\log\nel)$ leaves a partial correlation of $+0.27$
over all pairs and $+0.33$ over the heating pairs alone; residualising on a
full quadratic basis in the same two variables leaves $-0.44$
(\texttt{make\_ch5\_figures.py}, which refuses to draw
Figure~\ref{fig:M_vs_eps} at all unless the quadratic partial comes out
negative).

\textbf{Report the sign, not the magnitude.} The magnitude of the controlled
partial depends on the basis chosen for the control, which is a modelling
choice and not a measurement. \emph{[UNVERIFIED:
\texttt{thesis\_ready}~A11 records that the quadratic partial ran $-0.22$ to
$-0.70$ over eight scope and basis combinations. \texttt{make\_ch5\_figures.py}
computes four partials, not eight, and neither the eight combinations nor the
range appears in any script or artifact. What would close this: put the sweep
in the script and stamp its output.]}

The negative result itself is not new as a general statement.
Greenland concluded, for arbitrary collisional--radiative systems, that
validity criteria ``are not related to the equilibrium
time-scales''~\cite{Greenland2001b} and that ``the eigenvalues have secondary
importance''~\cite{Greenland2001a}. What this work adds is a quantification for one diagnostic and a
map of where the criterion misleads, which is a smaller claim than discovery and
a more useful one than assertion.

The reason the criterion fails here is visible in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_plateau_exact}. $\Msep$ compares two \emph{rates}; $\epsplat$
measures a \emph{distance}, the product of a sensitivity and a displacement,
neither of which appears in $\Msep$. A large $\Msep$ means the excited
states equilibrate quickly with the reservoir, which
Section~\ref{sec:qss_is_exact} confirms to a part in $10^{8}$. It says nothing
about whether the reservoir is in the right place.

\section{Where the quantitative scope begins}
\label{sec:scope}
% QUESTION: this chapter restricts quantitative claims to Te >= 2 eV. Is that a
% hedge, or a boundary the model sets for itself?
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Every quantitative statement in this chapter has been restricted to
$\Te\ge\SI{2}{\electronvolt}$. Two separate things go wrong below that
boundary. Neither of them was chosen, both were measured, and they put the
boundary in the same place.

\subsection{Holding the electron density fixed stops being consistent}
\label{sec:quasineutrality}

Every operator in this chapter is evaluated at fixed $\nel$: the temperature
steps, the density does not. That is not an approximation whose cost can be made
small by care, because in a hydrogen plasma the electrons and the neutrals are
not independent quantities. Quasineutrality ties them: every ground-state atom
that ionises delivers one electron, so if the reservoir collapses the electron
density must rise by the same absolute amount.

Whether that matters depends on how much of the hydrogen is neutral. At
$\Te = \benchTe$ the plasma is almost fully ionised and the neutral population
is a small perturbation on the electron budget, so the coupling is negligible.
At $\Te = \SI{1}{\electronvolt}$ the model's own steady state is $95$ to
\SI{98}{\percent} neutral. The ground state then holds an order of magnitude
more particles than the electron population does, and a modest fractional change
in it is an enormous fractional change in $\nel$.

Imposing conservation of nuclei across a single $+\SI{4.81}{\percent}$
temperature step makes this quantitative. Writing
$x = \ngs^{\mathrm{new}}/\ngs^{\mathrm{old}}$ for the fractional collapse of
the reservoir, the electron density that quasineutrality would require is:

The first two rows are not a separate piece of physics from the error itself.
$x = 0.506$ is $|\ln x| = 0.68$, and $0.68$ is the reservoir displacement that
\texttt{plateau\_gridmap.txt} records at $[0,4]$ and that
Section~\ref{sec:exact_decomposition} identifies as the source of the
\SI{38.7}{\percent}. \textbf{The displacement that produces the cold-edge error
is exactly the displacement that makes the fixed-density assumption
inconsistent.} Over the $392$ one-step operators, $68$ of them, \SI{17}{\percent},
require $|\Delta\nel/\nel| > \SI{10}{\percent}$, and $36$, \SI{9}{\percent},
require more than \SI{100}{\percent}. The model evaluates the post-step operator
at the old $\nel$ at every one of them. Above \SI{2}{\electronvolt} the required
correction never exceeds $5.4\times10^{-3}$, its largest value there sitting at
\SI{2.02}{\electronvolt}, and it first falls below $10^{-3}$ above
\SI{2.56}{\electronvolt} (\texttt{make\_story\_figures.py}). Five parts in a
thousand is small beside the tens of percent this chapter reports, so the
question does not arise above the boundary; it is not, however, the one part in
a thousand an earlier draft claimed.

This closes a gap between two objections that this thesis has until now
presented as separate and separately survivable. If the plasma parcel is closed,
quasineutrality forces $\nel$ up by a factor of $10$ to $20$ at the cold end,
and every rate coefficient in the post-step operator is then evaluated at the
wrong density. If instead the parcel is fed by transport, so that $\nel$
genuinely does stay fixed while the neutrals are consumed, then the ground state
is being resupplied from outside and is not stale, which removes the mechanism
that produces the error. \textbf{The two pictures cannot both be held, and the
cold edge needs one of them.}

The cold corner also fails a cruder test: it is not a divertor state. The
model's own steady solution at $[0,4]$ gives
$\ngs = \SI{1.47e15}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, which at a neutral temperature
of \SI{1}{\electronvolt} is a neutral pressure of \SI{235}{\pascal}, twelve
times the upper end of the ITER divertor design range of roughly $1$ to
\SI{20}{\pascal}; at $[0,7]$ it is \SI{3280}{\pascal}, $164$ times over. These
are fixed points of a zero-dimensional box with no transport and no pumping, and
they should be read as such. \emph{[SOURCE REQUIRED: the $1$ to
\SI{20}{\pascal} ITER divertor neutral-pressure range needs a citation to the
ITER divertor physics basis; the two computed pressures and the nuclei-balance
table above come from the collisional--radiative audit completed 10 September

their script and stamped artifact named.]}

\subsection{The plasma is not optically thin at the cold edge}
\label{sec:optical_depth}

The rate matrix of Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} treats the plasma as optically thin:
every photon emitted escapes. For the Balmer lines that is a good assumption
over the whole grid. For the Lyman series, which terminates on the ground state
where essentially all the atoms are, it fails badly at the cold edge. Computing
the Doppler line-centre optical depth from the model's own CRE solution gives a
Lyman-$\alpha$ depth of $114$ per centimetre at $\Te = \SI{1.00}{\electronvolt}$,
$\nel = \SI{5.18e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, falling to $0.036$ at
\SI{2.02}{\electronvolt} and $0.0023$ at \SI{2.95}{\electronvolt}
(\texttt{verify\_lyman\_trapping.py}). At the cold edge the Einstein

\textbf{Above \SI{2}{\electronvolt} nothing moves.} The ELM breakdown count
above \SI{2}{\electronvolt} stays at $45$ of $448$ for $D = 1$, $5$ and
\SI{20}{\centi\metre}, and the worst case runs $0.1748$, $0.1746$ and $0.1740$

range in a parameter this zero-dimensional model does not contain. At the
benchmark the plateau error moves by \SI{0.7}{\percent} over the same range.
\textbf{The $\Te\ge\SI{2}{\electronvolt}$ restriction is therefore not a hedge.
It is the measured boundary beyond which the neglected process most likely to
invalidate the result stops mattering}, which is a stronger statement than a
disclaimer and was not available before the calculation was run.

Below it, the cold-edge numbers are inflated. At $[0,4]$ the plateau error falls
from \SI{38.7}{\percent} to \SI{15.7}{\percent} at $D = \SI{1}{\centi\metre}$
and \SI{11.6}{\percent} at $D = \SI{20}{\centi\metre}$, a factor $2.5$ to
$3.3$, and which value inside that range it takes is set by the assumed slab
thickness rather than by anything the model computes. The point still exceeds
the \SI{10}{\percent} threshold at every thickness, so it still breaks down;
the \emph{magnitude} is not quotable. Below \SI{1.15}{\electronvolt} the density
column carrying the maximum wanders across three columns, a factor $7$, as a
function of $D$, so the two coldest rows cannot support a claim about where the
maximum is at all.

Chapter~\ref{ch:limits} takes up the mechanism of the fall, which is not a loss
of sensitivity, and the assumptions the slab geometry imports.

\subsection{The two boundaries coincide}

Radiation trapping and quasineutrality are unrelated pieces of physics, entering
through the Einstein coefficients and through the particle balance respectively.
They fail together. Above \SI{2}{\electronvolt} the trapped and untrapped
breakdown censuses are identical and the required density correction stays
below $5.4\times10^{-3}$; below it both grow quickly, and by \SI{1}{\electronvolt} the
first has divided the headline error by up to $3.3$ while the second has
invalidated the operator it was computed from.

Figure~\ref{fig:scope} draws both boundaries on the operating plane. The
quasineutrality contour at \SI{10}{\percent} sits at $\Te = 1.41$ to
\SI{1.50}{\electronvolt} and is almost independent of density. The
optical-depth contour at $\tau_0 = 1$ sits at $1.13$ to
\SI{1.98}{\electronvolt} at a slab thickness of \SI{5}{\centi\metre}, and
across the three thicknesses in the trapping sweep it spans $1.01$ to
\SI{2.36}{\electronvolt}. Of the 24 optical-depth crossings, 2 lie above
\SI{2}{\electronvolt}, both at the largest thickness $D = \SI{20}{\centi\metre}$
and at the two highest density columns.

\textbf{The $\Te\ge\SI{2}{\electronvolt}$ restriction is therefore not a hedge
and not a choice. It is the region on which this model's own assumptions are
mutually consistent, and it is where the neglected process most likely to
overturn the result has been shown not to.} Every headline number in this
chapter sits inside it. The cold-edge values are reported because they exist and
because suppressing them would be worse, and they are reported as what they are:
the asymptotic behaviour of a model outside its own domain of validity.

\section{What this chapter establishes}
\label{sec:results_summary}
% QUESTION: after all of the above, what can now be claimed about the cost of
% assuming ionisation balance that could not be claimed before?
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The approximation this thesis set out to test is not the one that fails.
Measured along a full 43-state trajectory, the quasi-steady-state closure is
accurate to $6.73\times10^{-9}$ at the very grid point carrying the largest
diagnostic error. The excited states keep up. What does not keep up is the
ground state, and the assumption a two-parameter lookup table makes about it is
not that the fast states equilibrate but that the plasma has finished changing.

What that assumption costs has a closed form. The plateau error factorises
exactly into a sensitivity and a reservoir displacement,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_plateau_exact}, and the displacement factorises again into a
reservoir gain and the size of the transient, Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_of_step}. The
gain moves by $5$ to \SI{6}{\percent} across a fourfold change in step size
while the error itself moves by a factor $4.9$, so the gain is the object worth
tabulating and the percentage is not. In the small-step limit the diagnostic
error per unit fractional temperature excursion is $|\overline{S}G|$: about
$1.3$ at the benchmark point and about $3.5$ at the worst point in the defended
range.

The sensitivity factor obeys a bound, $|\overline{S}| \le \tanh(|\Delta|/4) < 1$,
that follows from the structure of the linear system and not from any rate
coefficient. A line ratio cannot respond to its ground-state reservoir faster,
in relative terms, than the reservoir moves. At the crest the measured
sensitivity is at \SI{97}{\percent} of that bound.

Across the operating range the error has an interior maximum in density,
between $7\times10^{12}$ and $\SI{5e13}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$, and no
interior maximum in temperature. The density maximum is a position effect: the
separation between the two supply channels is nearly flat across density, and
what sweeps is the plasma's own reservoir sliding along a fixed sensitivity
curve, with the maximum where $\ln(u^{\mathrm{CRE}}/u^{\mathrm{peak}})$ changes
sign. The mechanism survived three independent tests, including a quantitative
prediction of how far the maximum moves when the shell pair changes, and it is a
property of the atomic system, needing no divertor geometry to produce it. It is
not detachment, and it is the $(3,4)$ pair's maximum rather than the Balmer
series'.

Inside the defensible scope, $\Te\ge\SI{2}{\electronvolt}$, $45$ of $448$
point-and-direction pairs commit an ELM-averaged error above \SI{10}{\percent}
at $\taudrive = \SI{100}{\micro\second}$, the worst being \SI{17.5}{\percent};
at citable divertor densities none do, the worst being \SI{7.2}{\percent}. That
census is unchanged across a twentyfold range in the one neglected process,
Lyman trapping, most likely to have invalidated it.

That scope boundary is the chapter's least expected result. It was adopted as a
precaution against radiation trapping and it turns out to be the boundary of
self-consistency: below \SI{2}{\electronvolt} the plasma is mostly neutral, and
conservation of nuclei then demands an electron-density change of $10$ to $20$
times across the same temperature step whose effect is being measured at fixed
$\nel$. The cold-edge numbers are asymptotic values of a model outside its own
domain, and this chapter reports them as such and builds nothing on them.

Finally, the criterion a modeller would reach for does not see any of this
coming. Timescale separation and diagnostic error have their maxima $52\times$
apart in density, and their apparent correlation reverses sign once the
operating point is controlled for. $\Msep$ compares rates; the diagnostic error
is a distance.

What the model cannot say is the subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:limits}: the plasma
is treated as optically thin where it is not, the atoms are held where they are
put, and the gas is treated as atomic where it is mostly molecular. Each of
those is quantified there rather than hedged.
